A peaceful day and a violent night

On Saturday 31 October 2026, a youth campaign called Gen Z regelt held demonstrations across Germany. It protested against the underfunding of schools, cuts to education, social services and culture, rising living costs and military conscription[1,2]. Rallies took place in more than 30 cities, including Berlin, Hamburg, Cologne, Munich and Leipzig[3,4,5]. The organisers said about 120,000 people took part, while police figures reported by the news site t-online put the total at more than 65,000[6,7]. The daytime marches were largely peaceful, but after dark there were clashes in several cities, and in Frankfurt am Main they turned into looting[3,8].

In Frankfurt, police counted about 8,000 marchers, while the organisers claimed 10,000[9]. Police dissolved the rally at about 6.20 p.m. because it had stopped being peaceful, after stones were thrown and fireworks set off[9,10]. Shop windows were smashed on and around the Zeil shopping street, and at least two shoe shops and a gun shop were looted[9]. By about 3 a.m. on Sunday the situation had calmed[9,11].

Reuters reported that 17 stores were damaged and five looted, that 1,300 officers had been deployed and more than 100 injured, and that 54 people aged between 13 and 29 had been arrested[1]. Police checked the identities of 366 people and ordered 1,225 to leave the area[12].

A loosely organised movement and its demands

Gen Z regelt emerged recently through social media[13]. According to the Berlin daily taz, its organising team was made up of about 30 people aged 18 to 28 from different cities who had met online, and its main spokesperson was Akram Selo, an 18-year-old school-leaver[13]. The campaign set out six demands, including money for schools instead of cuts, an affordable life, no military conscription and immediate climate protection[5]. Its founding statement rejected violence against people or property, and Selo told taz that the group opposed right-wing and left-wing extremism alike and was not tied to any party[13]. The Federal Student Conference supported investment in education but kept its distance from the campaign; its secretary-general, Amy Kirchhoff, said nobody knew exactly who was behind it[14].

The campaign declared its solidarity with the school students' protests in France[5]. The French movement began on 17 September 2026 at a high school in Créteil, a suburb of Paris, over teacher shortages, overcrowded classes and run-down buildings, and widened to include living costs[15,16]. On 17 October, police in France arrested more than 1,800 people after fires and looting[17].

Conscription, prices and school buildings

On 5 December 2025, the Bundestag passed the Military Service Modernisation Act by 323 votes to 272, with one abstention[18]. The law took effect on 1 January 2026[19]. Service remains voluntary, but if the defence situation or the staffing of the armed forces requires it, the government can ask the Bundestag to pass a new law bringing in conscription as needed[19,20]. According to France 24, protesters feared that the new voluntary model was a step towards bringing back conscription[6].

The economic complaints can be measured. The Federal Statistical Office's preliminary estimate puts consumer price inflation in October 2026 at 2.8% compared with a year earlier, with energy prices up 12.2%[21]. On schools, the development bank KfW put the investment backlog reported by municipalities at €231.2bn, of which €68.9bn was in school buildings[22]. It is, however, a perceived backlog reported by municipal finance officers, from a survey with a response rate of 37%[22,23].

Contested counts and competing political readings

Police estimated that they faced between 1,000 and 1,500 violent participants in Frankfurt[1,10]. Taz reported that up to 100 people rioted and looted in the city centre, and Hesse's interior minister, Roman Poseck, blamed about 100 left-wing extremists together with young people prepared to use violence[10,12]. Outside Frankfurt, the picture was mixed. In Berlin, 27 officers suffered minor injuries and 56 people were arrested, while the main rally at the Brandenburg Gate went ahead almost undisturbed[8]. In Hamburg, barricades were set alight and police used water cannon and tear gas[3,4]. Nationwide, at least 250 people were arrested[7,24].

Poseck also said that the perpetrators had been predominantly of migrant background and that there should be no false taboos on the subject[7,24]. The federal interior minister, Alexander Dobrindt of the CSU, told the newspaper Bild that violent left-wing groups and troublemakers from the migrant milieu had taken over peaceful demonstrations[24]. Police said most of those arrested were German nationals[1,7]. Frankfurt's mayor, Mike Josef, noted that many of the officers who had protected the city had a migration history themselves[25].

Politicians also disagreed about what the protests said about young people. The federal education minister, Karin Prien (CDU), condemned the violence but called young people's concerns justified and highly relevant[7,24]. Johannes Winkel, leader of the Junge Union, said the government was neglecting the young generation and that this was a fact, not a vague feeling[26]. Gen Z regelt said that violence had never been part of the movement[12]. A spokesperson identified only as Amadeo called the day a first step and announced that the campaign would join Europe-wide protests on 20 November[4,8,25].

What remains uncertain

Most of the numbers from the night are provisional statements made within a day of the events. Police and organiser estimates of turnout differ by roughly a factor of two[6,7]. The published reporting gives no breakdown of those arrested beyond their ages and the fact that most hold German citizenship[1,7,24]. The damage in Frankfurt has not been costed[11]. The Federal Student Conference has criticised the lack of transparency of Gen Z regelt[13,14]. The next test comes on 20 November, when Poseck has promised a robust police response to future protests[12].

1A peaceful afternoon and a violent night in Frankfurt

On Saturday 31 October 2026, a youth campaign called Gen Z regelt, which Reuters translates as "Gen Z takes charge", held demonstrations across Germany. It protested against the underfunding of schools, cuts to education, social services and culture, rising living costs and military conscription[1,15]. Rallies took place in more than 30 cities, including Berlin, Hamburg, Cologne, Munich and Leipzig[2,3,4]. The organisers said about 120,000 people took part, while police figures reported by the news site t-online put the total at more than 65,000[6,7]. The daytime marches were largely peaceful, but after dark there were clashes in several cities, and in Frankfurt am Main they turned into looting[2,5].

In Frankfurt, police counted about 8,000 marchers, while the organisers claimed 10,000[16]. Police dissolved the rally at about 6.20 p.m. because it had stopped being peaceful, after stones were thrown and fireworks set off[8,16]. Over the following hours, shop windows were smashed on and around the Zeil shopping street, and at least two shoe shops and a gun shop were looted; police secured two weapons at the gun shop and made several arrests[16]. Petrol bombs, rocks and bottles were thrown at the emergency services, and police responded with water cannon, armoured vehicles, a helicopter and special operations units[1,16]. By about 3 a.m. on Sunday the situation had calmed[14,16].

Reuters reported that 17 stores were damaged and five looted, that 1,300 officers had been deployed and more than 100 injured, and that 54 people aged between 13 and 29 had been arrested[1]. One officer lost several teeth after being hit in the face by a stone[8,13]. Police checked the identities of 366 people and ordered 1,225 to leave the area[13]. In Frankfurt, 87 criminal charges were recorded[7]. Frankfurt police appealed for photographs and video of property damage, theft and serious assaults on officers around the Hauptwache, Konstablerwache and Zeil[17].

The night ran against forecasts made before the protests. Jannis Julien Grimm, a researcher at the Free University of Berlin's Centre for Interdisciplinary Peace and Conflict Research, had expected the demonstrations to stay largely peaceful. He argued that Germany has a significantly less confrontational protest culture[4]. According to WirtschaftsWoche, the federal interior ministry had reported no concrete calls to violence[4]. Within a day, the political argument had moved from the protesters' demands to who the rioters were.

2A loosely organised movement with six demands

Gen Z regelt emerged recently through social media[18]. According to the Berlin daily taz, its organising team was made up of about 30 people aged 18 to 28 from different cities who had met online. Its main spokesperson was Akram Selo, an 18-year-old school-leaver[18]. WirtschaftsWoche reported that Selo was named in the legal notice on the campaign's website with a Swiss address, and that the campaign mobilised through TikTok, Instagram and Telegram[4]. The influencer Gianni Matheja, who was briefly a spokesperson, withdrew shortly before the protests, citing disagreements over aims and methods[4,18].

The campaign set out six demands: money for schools, addiction support and psychotherapy instead of cuts; an affordable life; a fair sharing of burdens; no military conscription; protection of fundamental rights against wider surveillance; and immediate climate protection[4]. Its founding statement rejected violence against people or property. Selo told taz that the group opposed right-wing and left-wing extremism alike and was not tied to any party[18]. The youth wings of the Greens and the Left party supported the protests. Taz reported that established far-left cadre groups such as Kommunistischer Aufbau were involved in some local organising, but noted that earlier protests against conscription had been entirely peaceful[18]. The Federal Student Conference supported investment in education but kept its distance from the campaign; its secretary-general, Amy Kirchhoff, said nobody knew exactly who was behind it[19].

Protesters described their complaints in concrete terms. "At my school, the ceiling is collapsing because of water damage, nothing is being done about it, we have a shortage of teachers," Janelle Schulz, a student from Berlin, told CBS News[2]. Lina Friedrich, a spokesperson for the campaign quoted by NBC News, said: "We are here today to fight for peace and freedom"[15]. In Berlin, some protesters carried banners calling for the resignation of Chancellor Friedrich Merz and the French president, Emmanuel Macron[20].

The campaign declared its solidarity with the school students' protests in France[4]. The French movement began on 17 September 2026 at a high school in Créteil, a suburb of Paris, over teacher shortages, overcrowded classes and run-down buildings, and widened to include living costs[21,22]. On 17 October, French authorities counted about 550,000 demonstrators and organisers about 800,000, and police arrested more than 1,800 people after fires and looting[23]. The "Gen Z" label had earlier been used for youth-led uprisings in Kenya in 2024 and in Nepal in September 2025, where protests brought down the government[24].

3Conscription, rearmament and the state of German schools

Conscription has a particular history in Germany. About eight million young men served as conscripts between 1957 and 2011. From 1 July 2011 young men were no longer called up, and the Bundeswehr became an all-volunteer force[25]. On 18 March 2025, the Bundestag voted by 512 to 206 to amend the constitution. Defence spending above 1% of GDP was exempted from the debt brake, and a €500bn fund was created for infrastructure and climate investment over 12 years[26].

On 5 December 2025, the Bundestag passed the Military Service Modernisation Act by 323 votes to 272, with one abstention. The governing CDU/CSU and SPD voted for it, and the AfD, the Greens and the Left voted against[27]. The law took effect on 1 January 2026[10]. All 18-year-olds now receive a questionnaire about their motivation and suitability for service, which men must answer and women may answer if they choose. From 1 July 2027, men born in 2008 or later must undergo a medical assessment[10]. Volunteers are paid at least €2,600 a month before tax, including accommodation[11]. The government's target is 260,000 active personnel and 200,000 reservists by 2035[10].

Service remains voluntary, but the law contains a fallback. If the defence situation or the staffing of the armed forces requires it, the government can ask the Bundestag to pass a new law bringing in conscription as needed, known as Bedarfswehrpflicht[10,11]. Defence Minister Pistorius argued when the law passed that far more young people were willing to take on responsibility than parts of parliament or the public believed[28]. According to France 24, protesters feared that the new voluntary model was a step towards bringing back conscription[6].

The economic complaints can be measured. The Federal Statistical Office's preliminary estimate puts consumer price inflation in October 2026 at 2.8% compared with a year earlier, with energy prices up 12.2% and core inflation, which excludes food and energy, at 2.1%[29]. On schools, the development bank KfW put the investment backlog reported by municipalities at €231.2bn, of which €68.9bn was in school buildings[12]. That total was a new high. It is, however, a perceived backlog reported by municipal finance officers, from a survey of 2,900 municipalities and districts in the first quarter of 2026 with a response rate of 37%[12]. Across the EU, a Eurobarometer online survey of 25,863 people aged 16 to 30, carried out between 25 September and 3 October 2024, found that rising prices and the cost of living were the main concern, named by 40%[30].

4Counting crowds, rioters and arrests

Almost every key number from the night comes from a party with a stake in how it is read: the police and interior ministries on one side, the organisers on the other. Participation is the clearest case. The organisers' own count was about 120,000, and some reports gave it as more than 100,000[5,6]. According to the Berliner Kurier, the campaign had registered 184,000 participants across 34 cities, while NBC News reported an initial expectation of 182,000[3,15]. Police figures cited by t-online came to more than 65,000[7]. In Frankfurt, the organisers had told the authorities to expect about 1,700 people; police counted about 8,000[16,31].

Estimates of how many people rioted differ even more. Police estimated that they faced between 1,000 and 1,500 violent participants in Frankfurt[1,8]. Taz reported that up to 100 people rioted and looted in the city centre. Poseck blamed about 100 left-wing extremists together with young people prepared to use violence[8,13]. The figures may describe a hard core and a wider crowd, but none of the reports explains how they were reached. Accounts of the timing also differ. Police dissolved the rally at about 6.20 p.m., and taz placed the start of the violence near Bleichstrasse shortly before 6.30 p.m. Hessenschau's live coverage, by contrast, described crowds of young people confronting police from about 9 p.m.[8,13,14].

Outside Frankfurt, the picture was mixed. In Berlin, where 1,500 officers were deployed, 27 officers suffered minor injuries and 56 people were arrested, while the main rally at the Brandenburg Gate went ahead almost undisturbed[5]. In Cologne, police banned the planned march because of masked participants and fireworks. About 200 people threw bottles, firecrackers and bicycles at officers near the cathedral, and five were taken into custody[3]. Cologne's police chief, Johannes Hermanns, said that without intervention there would have been scenes like those in Paris or Milan[3]. In Hamburg, where police counted about 6,700 marchers, barricades were set alight and police used water cannon and tear gas[2,3]. In Leipzig, up to 200 people threw road signs onto the road and damaged bus stops after the rally[3]. Nationwide, at least 250 people were arrested[7,9].

The damage has not yet been costed. Hessenschau reported that the authorities spoke of substantial damage but would not give figures until inspections were complete[14]. Most of these numbers are provisional statements made within 24 hours of the events, and none has been tested in court.

5Ministers point to extremists and migrant backgrounds

Hesse's interior minister, Roman Poseck (CDU), had warned before the protests that left-wing groups would try to exploit them. He said the police would tolerate no violence against people or property[13,31]. Afterwards he said: "I am shocked by what I have seen, and there is no point in sugar-coating it. Let me make this very clear: Things cannot go on like this"[1]. He described a devastating combination of left-wing extremists and violent young people[14]. Hesse's premier, Boris Rhein, said he was shocked and that those responsible would face consequences[16,32].

Poseck also said that the perpetrators had been predominantly of migrant background and that there should be no false taboos on the subject[7,9]. The federal interior minister, Alexander Dobrindt of the CSU, told the newspaper Bild that violent left-wing groups and troublemakers from the migrant milieu had taken over peaceful demonstrations[9]. In remarks reported by Reuters, Dobrindt said: "I expect there to be severe penalties. We are not prepared to accept, in the slightest, that such incidents and such rioting should take place here"[1]. He also credited the police with preventing outbreaks of violence like those in Paris[9].

The public evidence on this point is limited. Police said most of those arrested were German nationals[1,7]. The reports available on 1 November did not explain how the ministers had established the rioters' backgrounds, or whether the claim referred to those arrested or to the wider crowd. A national baseline helps to interpret it. The Federal Statistical Office reports that 26.3% of the population had an immigration history in 2025, meaning that they, or both of their parents, had moved to Germany since 1950[33]. The share was 28.9% among people under 20 and 36% among 25 to 34-year-olds[33,34]. These are national figures; the data presented by the Mediendienst Integration give no breakdown for Hesse or Frankfurt[34].

Others urged caution. Frankfurt's mayor, Mike Josef, noted that many of the officers who had protected the city had a migration history themselves[35]. In a commentary for hessenschau, the reporter Frank Angermund wrote that violence was a matter of personal responsibility, not origin, and warned that right-wing groups could exploit the images[36]. Andreas Roßkopf of the police union GdP described the daytime protests as very peaceful and blamed the later violence on autonomous, partly masked individuals[5,35].

6Competing readings of a generational protest

Beyond the policing, politicians disagreed about what the protests said about young people. The federal education minister, Karin Prien (CDU), condemned the violence but called young people's concerns justified and highly relevant[7,9]. Dagmar Schmidt, deputy leader of the SPD parliamentary group, proposed a national education summit with the states and municipalities[9]. Johannes Winkel, leader of the Junge Union, the CDU's youth organisation, said the government was neglecting the young generation and that this was a fact, not a vague feeling. He linked the point to pension reform and fairness between generations[37].

Opposition politicians drew a line between the protesters and the rioters. The Greens leader Franziska Brantner said violence harmed the legitimate cause of those who had protested peacefully[8]. Ines Schwerdtner of the Left party argued that neither the affordability crisis, nor social cuts, nor the wish for peaceful protest would disappear because small groups caused disorder afterwards[9].

The organisers distanced themselves from the violence. In a statement reported by taz, Gen Z regelt said it stood for peaceful protest, democratic values and freedom of expression, and that violence had never been part of the movement[13]. A spokesperson identified only as Amadeo said the campaign had had no influence over what happened after the rallies. He called the day a first step and announced that the campaign would join Europe-wide protests on 20 November[3,5,35]. The Frankfurt organiser Mubarik Sabir said he was deeply saddened that the violence could not be prevented[16]. Poseck has promised a robust police response to future protests, naming the 20 November date[13]. The Federal Student Conference plans its own protest in Berlin that day with the Education and Science Union (GEW), the Federal Parents' Council and Fridays for Future[19].

France offers a point of comparison. After the riots of 17 October, President Macron said: "Those who looted will have to pay back for the damage, and those who sought to bring down the Republic will be held accountable"[23]. Reuters noted that later French protests had been largely peaceful[1]. On 31 October, French turnout was 320,000 according to the government and 500,000 according to organisers[5]. The Council on Foreign Relations reported that 55% of French voters supported the movement in October. It added that later polling put Marine Le Pen of the National Rally above 30% ahead of the presidential election due in April 2027[21].

Uncertainties and open questions

Several central facts rest on provisional police and ministerial statements made within a day of the events. Police put the number of violent participants in Frankfurt at 1,000 to 1,500, while taz reported about 100 rioters and Poseck spoke of about 100 left-wing extremists within a larger group[1,8,13]. The claim that the perpetrators were mainly of migrant background comes from Poseck and Dobrindt. The published reporting gives no breakdown of those arrested beyond their ages and the fact that most hold German citizenship[1,7,9]. Police and organiser estimates of turnout differ by roughly a factor of two[6,7]. The damage in Frankfurt has not been costed[14].

How Gen Z regelt is organised is also unclear. The Federal Student Conference has criticised its lack of transparency, and taz reported that, at the time, the central team directly supported only five of 24 planned rallies[18,19]. The background data have limits of their own. The KfW school backlog is an estimate reported by the municipalities themselves, and the October inflation figure is preliminary[12,29]. One report suggests that most of the rioters were not part of the peaceful march, but this has not been established[16].

Conclusion

Tens of thousands of young people marched in more than 30 German cities on 31 October 2026, largely peacefully, against underfunded schools, living costs and conscription[1,2]. In Frankfurt, the night that followed left 17 stores damaged, more than 100 police officers injured and 54 people aged 13 to 29 under arrest[1]. Hessenschau reported that most of the rioters had not taken part in the peaceful march[16].

The federal and Hessian interior ministers have presented the violence as the work of left-wing extremists and people of migrant background. The education minister and opposition politicians have stressed that the underlying complaints remain[7,8,9]. Those complaints have measurable counterparts, from a €68.9bn backlog in school buildings to a conscription law that allows a return to compulsory service[10,12]. The next test comes on 20 November, when Gen Z regelt and the Federal Student Conference both plan protests and Hesse has promised a robust police response[13,19]. Which reading prevails is likely to shape how far the government engages with the demands themselves.
