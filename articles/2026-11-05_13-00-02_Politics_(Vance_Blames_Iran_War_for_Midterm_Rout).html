A vice president blames the war for defeat

Vice President JD Vance wrote on the social media platform X on Wednesday 4 November 2026 that "The reason we lost is the war", a day after Republicans lost control of both chambers of Congress in the midterm elections[1,2,3]. He added that "many people who voted for Trump/Vance in 2024 voted for Democrats in 2026"[3,4]. Vance named four concerns behind that shift: ending the war, the cost of living and inflation, putting America first, and changing the relationship with Israel[2,5].

President Donald Trump accepted that the war had cost his party votes but rejected any suggestion that launching it had been a mistake[1,2]. "Iran was a piece of it. I don't think about it. I did the right thing," he said, according to NBC News[2]. Asked about Vance's criticism, Trump said, according to ABC News, "If I had it to do again, I'd have to do it again because long term, it's the only answer", and added: "You can't let Iran have a nuclear weapon"[4].

The disagreement matters because Democrats will control Congress next year[6]. Trump has said he would use his veto against their legislation[6]. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer said on 4 November that "This war must end" and pointed to Congress's power of the purse as leverage[7,8].

The scale of the Republican defeat

By late on 4 November, CNBC counted seven Republican-held Senate seats won by Democrats, in Kansas, Texas, Iowa, South Carolina, Ohio, Maine and North Carolina, while Republicans had not taken a single Democratic-held seat[9]. CBS News expected Democrats to hold at least 54 Senate seats and estimated that they would control between 251 and 268 House seats[3]. Connor Echols, managing editor of Responsible Statecraft, called the result "one of the largest midterm blowouts in decades"[5].

The NBC News exit poll put Trump's approval among voters at 38%, compared with 45% at the 2018 midterms during his first term[10]. The CBS News exit poll, which also put his approval at 38%, called it "the lowest approval rating for a president in a midterm election"[11]. In the NBC poll, 75% of voters said the economy was in rough shape and 44% said their finances were worse than two years earlier[10].

What the polls measured and what they did not

Several surveys agree that most voters judged the war a mistake. The NBC News exit poll found that 63% said the United States had made the wrong decision in taking military action against Iran[10]. The Associated Press voter poll, reported by PBS NewsHour, put the figure at about 6 in 10, with nearly all Democratic House voters calling the decision wrong and roughly 8 in 10 Republican House voters calling it right[12]. The CBS News exit poll found that about a quarter of Republicans thought the decision wrong, and that the voters most worried about affording gasoline were the most likely to disagree with the attack on Iran[11].

The polls disagree more on how far the war moved votes. According to ABC News, 34% of House voters placed the war in the highest category of importance for their vote, against 49% for the cost of living and 55% for government corruption[4]. PBS NewsHour similarly reported that only about one third of voters gave the war that rating[12]. Responsible Statecraft reported that 74% of voters called the war at least a major factor, without naming the pollster[13].

Opposition to the war began before the campaign. Gallup found in a telephone survey of 1,001 adults conducted from 1 to 15 June that 34% approved of the US military action against Iran, against 76% initial approval for the 2003 Iraq war[14]. A Pew Research Center survey conducted from 23 to 29 March found that 35% were confident in Trump's decisions on Iran and 64% were not[15].

Fuel prices as the link between war and ballot

The US Energy Information Administration (EIA) put the average retail price of regular gasoline at $4.153 a gallon on 2 November, $1.134 higher than a year earlier, and diesel at $6.292, up $2.539[16]. Its October outlook assumed that flows through the Strait of Hormuz would remain constrained through the final quarter of 2026 and forecast Brent crude at $105 a barrel for that quarter[17].

Public concern rose with these prices. Pew found that the share of adults very concerned about gas and energy prices rose from 43% in January, before the conflict, to 60% in July and 74% in a survey conducted from 28 September to 4 October[18]. Mark Zandi, chief economist at Moody's Analytics, estimated that the war had cost the average household at least $1,200 over its first six months, with energy alone accounting for around $700 per household[19]. The New Yorker columnist John Cassidy, who reported the figure, cautioned that it was a rough sketch of scale rather than a precise calculation[19].

Competing readings and what remains uncertain

Senator Ted Cruz said the war "should have been brief, decisive, and over"[8]. Foreign Policy wrote that Vance, a potential 2028 presidential candidate, appeared to have decided that defending an unpopular war carried more political risk than breaking with the president[1]. Among the war's supporters, Mark Levin rejected responsibility for the losses, insisting "it is not my fault!", while Will Chamberlain conceded that it was "clearly catastrophically unpopular"[20].

Iran's leaders presented the result as a vote against the war. Parliament speaker and chief negotiator Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf wrote that "Americans said NO TO WAR, and we endorse this message"[21,22]. Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei warned that "should such aggressions be repeated, the war's scope will become much wider"[7].

The claim that the war caused the defeat rests on patterns in survey data rather than on any direct measure of cause. About one third of voters gave the war top importance in the Associated Press and ABC News data, while Responsible Statecraft cited 74% calling it at least a major factor, from a pollster it did not name[4,12,13]. Nine House races were uncalled late on 4 November[9]. Trump has said he would "do it again" and that the United States is "locked and loaded if we need to go", and Iran's Supreme Leader has warned of wider escalation[7].

2The scale of the Republican defeat

Voters went to the polls on Tuesday 3 November[4]. By late on 4 November, CNBC counted seven Republican-held Senate seats won by Democrats, in Kansas, Texas, Iowa, South Carolina, Ohio, Maine and North Carolina, while Republicans had not taken a single Democratic-held seat[6]. CBS News expected Democrats to hold at least 54 Senate seats and estimated that they would control between 251 and 268 House seats[3]. CNBC reported that Democrats had captured at least 24 House districts, with nine House races still uncalled late on Wednesday[6].

The gains went beyond Congress. Democrats won five governorships, including Georgia, Iowa, Ohio and Nevada, giving them a majority of the nation's governorships for the first time since 2010, and took as many as 12 state legislative chambers, according to CNBC[6]. In individual races, Responsible Statecraft reported that Joshua Turek defeated Ashley Hinson in Iowa by nearly 5 percentage points, that Roy Cooper beat Michael Whatley in North Carolina, and that Abdul El-Sayed led Mike Rogers in Michigan by 52% to 46% with 95% of votes counted[14]. Echols called the result "one of the largest midterm blowouts in decades"[15].

Exit polls found the president in a weak position. The NBC News exit poll put Trump's approval among voters at 38%, compared with 45% at the 2018 midterms during his first term[7]. The CBS News exit poll, which also put his approval at 38%, called it "the lowest approval rating for a president in a midterm election" and found it lower than George W. Bush's in 2006[8]. In the NBC poll, 75% of voters said the economy was in rough shape, 44% said their finances were worse than two years earlier, and 39% named the economy as their most important issue[7].

These figures show how large the defeat was, but not what caused it. Vance's own explanation rested partly on his reading of turnout: he said the Florida electorate was 9 points more Republican than Democratic, "just a couple points worse than 2024"[3]. Trump treated the result as a verdict on his party rather than on himself, saying he would have won had he been on the ballot[2]. Testing either account means looking closely at what the polls measured and at how the war reached voters' household budgets.

3Eight months of war and a failed settlement

The war began on 28 February 2026 with joint United States and Israeli air strikes on Iran that killed the Supreme Leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, in his Tehran office, together with dozens of senior political and military leaders[17]. Iran retaliated at once against Israel, United States facilities and regional allies, and from 1 March began striking commercial tankers to obstruct shipping through the Strait of Hormuz[17]. Mojtaba Khamenei became Supreme Leader in early March, following the killing of his father[18,19]. The Pentagon calls the period from 28 February to 6 July Operation Epic Fury[20]. Trump has defended the war on the ground that Iran cannot be allowed a nuclear weapon[5].

A settlement came close in June. On 17 June the two governments signed a memorandum of understanding that provided for a halt to military operations on all fronts including Lebanon, the lifting of the US naval blockade within 30 days, Iranian mine clearance and 60 days of safe passage through the Strait of Hormuz, the lifting of sanctions on Iran and a $300 billion reconstruction package, while Iran reaffirmed that it would not pursue nuclear weapons[21]. The deal unravelled within weeks: the vessel Ever Lovely was struck near the coast of Oman on 25 June, the United States launched counter-strikes on 27 June, Trump declared the agreement "over" on 7 July, and it formally expired on 17 August[21]. Al Jazeera's analysis traced the collapse to a dispute over shipping routes, vague wording on Israel and an unfunded reconstruction pledge[21].

Congress tried to halt the war and failed. Both chambers approved a war powers resolution in June that the White House disregarded, and on 25 September the Senate defeated a further resolution by 49 votes to 50, with four Republicans voting in favour and one Democrat, John Fetterman, voting against[22]. On election day, US forces struck Iran's Qeshm Island, Al Jazeera reported[4].

The human cost is documented unevenly. Stars and Stripes reported that the Pentagon's Defense Casualty Analysis System listed 23 US service members killed and 865 wounded as of late October, including 8 deaths and 448 wounded since 7 July[20]. Democracy Now reported that an earlier revision, from 18 to 21 deaths, was made without public announcement[23]. For Iran, the Human Rights Activists News Agency (HRANA), an independent Iranian rights organisation, counted 3,636 deaths including 1,701 civilians as of 7 April, Reuters reported[24]. Iran's health ministry has reported 3,528 deaths, World Health Organization and UN humanitarian office figures give 3,375, and the rights group Hengaw counted 7,650 by 8 April, according to a Wikipedia compilation of these sources[25].

4What the polls measured and what they did not

Several separate surveys agree that most voters judged the war a mistake. The NBC News exit poll, which combined interviews at polling places with phone, mail and online interviews of early and postal voters, found that 63% said the United States had made the wrong decision in taking military action against Iran[7]. The Associated Press voter poll, reported by PBS NewsHour, put the figure at about 6 in 10, with nearly all Democratic House voters calling the decision wrong and roughly 8 in 10 Republican House voters calling it right[13]. The CBS News exit poll found that about a quarter of Republicans thought the decision wrong, and that the voters most worried about affording gasoline were the most likely to disagree with the attack on Iran[8].

The polls disagree more on how far the war moved votes. According to ABC News, 34% of House voters placed the war in the highest category of importance for their vote, against 49% for the cost of living and 55% for government corruption[5]. PBS NewsHour similarly reported that only about one third of voters gave the war that rating, and that the cost of living and corruption ranked higher[13]. Responsible Statecraft reported that 74% of voters called the war at least a major factor and that 62% thought the use of force wrong, without naming the pollster[14].

Opposition to the war began well before the campaign. Gallup found in a telephone survey of 1,001 adults conducted from 1 to 15 June that 34% approved of the US military action against Iran, with a margin of error of plus or minus 4 percentage points at the 95% confidence level[26]. Approval stood at 79% among Republicans, 26% among independents and 8% among Democrats, and Gallup set the result against 76% initial approval for the 2003 Iraq war[26]. A Pew Research Center survey of 3,507 adults conducted from 23 to 29 March found that 35% were confident in Trump's decisions on Iran and 64% were not, and that rising fuel costs were the public's leading concern about the war[27].

Two limitations follow from these data. Exit polls record how voters describe their own motives, not the causal weight of any single issue. The questions about importance are also worded differently by each pollster, which may explain why estimates of the war's importance run from about one third to nearly three quarters of voters. The CBS finding that fuel worries and opposition to the war go together suggests that the war and the cost of living are overlapping explanations rather than the rival ones that Trump's and Vance's accounts imply.

5Fuel prices as the link between war and ballot

The US Energy Information Administration (EIA) put the average retail price of regular gasoline at $4.153 a gallon on 2 November, $1.134 higher than a year earlier, and diesel at $6.292, up $2.539[9]. In April the EIA said the closure of the Strait of Hormuz was forcing Middle Eastern producers to shut in output, estimated those shut-ins at 7.5 million barrels per day in March, and warned that full restoration of flows would take months[28]. Its October outlook estimated that shut-ins peaked at 10.9 million barrels per day in May and fell to 4.8 million in September[29]. The same outlook assumed that flows would remain constrained through the final quarter of 2026 and forecast Brent crude at $105 a barrel for that quarter, $14 above the previous month's forecast[29]. Markets moved after the vote: CBS News reported that Brent fell below $100 a barrel on 4 November for the first time since September, to about $94[12].

Public concern rose with these prices. Pew found that the share of adults very concerned about gas and energy prices rose from 43% in January, before the conflict, to 60% in July and 74% in a survey of 5,062 adults conducted from 28 September to 4 October[10]. Trump argued that the pain would be temporary: "As soon as the war ends, fuel prices are going to drop like a rock"[2].

Estimates of the war's wider cost depend on how they are calculated. Mark Zandi, chief economist at Moody's Analytics, estimated that the war had cost the average household at least $1,200 over its first six months, adding together higher energy costs, effects on inflation and interest rates, and military spending, with energy alone accounting for around $700 per household[30]. The New Yorker columnist John Cassidy, who reported the figure, cautioned that it was a rough sketch of scale rather than a precise calculation, given how little the Pentagon discloses about the war's costs[30]. The Arab Center Washington DC cited military costs of $33.4 billion as of 29 June and consumer price inflation of 3.4% in the year to August, while Al Jazeera put the war's cost to US taxpayers at $47 billion[4,31].

The two direct-cost figures refer to different dates, and neither source sets out a full method. They are therefore better read as points along a rising curve than as competing claims. Taken together with the polling, the price data suggest a clear route by which a distant war could reach voters: through the cost of filling a car and of moving goods by diesel-powered transport.

6Competing readings in Washington and Tehran

Not all Republicans accept Vance's diagnosis. Senator Ted Cruz said the war "should have been brief, decisive, and over" and that Washington "should never, under any circumstances, give the Ayatollah billions of dollars"[4]. Paul Musgrave of Georgetown University in Qatar told Al Jazeera that the war had exposed major internal divisions inside the party, while the former Republican congressional staffer John Feehery counted it as a big part of the defeat alongside energy prices, healthcare and corruption[4]. Echols wrote that Secretary of State Marco Rubio, who largely avoided campaign events, had emerged as a stronger 2028 contender[15].

The war's supporters gave different answers after the vote, Responsible Statecraft reported. The commentator Mark Levin, who had called the war a "4 percent issue" for voters in September, rejected responsibility for the losses[32]. Will Chamberlain of the Article III Project, who had called the war a "geopolitical masterstroke", conceded that it was "clearly catastrophically unpopular"[32]. Rebeccah Heinrichs of the Hudson Institute blamed advocates of diplomacy rather than the war's supporters for the losses[32].

Iran's leaders presented the result as a vote against the war. Parliament speaker and chief negotiator Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf wrote that "Americans said NO TO WAR, and we endorse this message", warning that if Washington ignored that will, "it will learn our NO comes with a Nightmare"[19,33]. Judiciary chief Gholam-Hossein Mohseni-Ejei said: "Trump failed both in war and in politics. We will certainly impose even more defeats upon him"[33]. Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei said that anyone hoping for a change in US behaviour was "gravely mistaken" and warned that "should such aggressions be repeated, the war's scope will become much wider"[12,33]. At a rally in Tehran on Wednesday marking the 1979 seizure of the US embassy, demonstrators burned a banner depicting Trump, Vance, Benjamin Netanyahu and Steve Witkoff, and one participant said the Democratic sweep would make little difference to US policy towards Iran[34].

Other governments drew their own conclusions. The European Union's top diplomat, Kaja Kallas, hoped the new Congress would strengthen support for Ukraine and increase pressure on Russia, while Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov expressed hope that Trump could continue his "peace-making efforts"[18]. Giorgio Cafiero of Gulf State Analytics told Al Jazeera that Trump "prides himself in leaving everyone, including the Iranians, constantly guessing" and could yet escalate the crisis significantly[4].

Uncertainties and open questions

The central claim, that the war caused the Republican defeat, rests on patterns in survey data rather than on any direct measure of cause. The polls disagree on how important the war was to voters: about one third gave it top importance in the Associated Press and ABC News data, while Responsible Statecraft cited 74% calling it at least a major factor, from a pollster it did not name[5,13,14]. Vance's argument that Republican voters switched sides rather than stayed at home rests on his own reading of turnout, such as his claim that the Florida electorate was 9 points more Republican than Democratic[3]. The results are also incomplete: nine House races were uncalled late on 4 November, and CBS News gave a range of 251 to 268 Democratic House seats[3,6].

On the war itself, the most widely cited independent count of Iranian deaths, by HRANA, dates from April, and the totals published by Iran's health ministry, UN agencies and rights groups differ by thousands[24,25]. The official US military toll has been revised upwards without public announcement[23]. Estimates of the war's economic cost are, by the account of those who reported them, rough[30]. Trump has said he would "do it again", and Iran's Supreme Leader has warned of wider escalation, so the vote's consequences for the conflict are still uncertain[12].

Conclusion

The evidence supports a narrower conclusion than either Vance or Trump offers. Majorities in the main exit polls called the war a wrong decision, opposition was strongest among voters worried about fuel costs, and gasoline and diesel prices on the eve of the vote were far above their level a year earlier[7,8,9]. Yet fewer voters ranked the war as their top concern than ranked the cost of living or corruption[5]. The war and household costs appear to have worked together rather than as competing explanations.

What changes now is the balance of power in Washington: Democrats will control Congress next year, and Schumer has pointed to its power over spending[11,12]. Trump says he would fight the war again, while Tehran warns that renewed attacks would widen it[5,12]. The open question is whether the new Congress can shorten a war that the White House has continued despite earlier congressional votes against it[22].
