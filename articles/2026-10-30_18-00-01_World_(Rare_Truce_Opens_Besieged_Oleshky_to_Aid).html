A joint convoy under a local ceasefire

On 30 October 2026, a convoy of buses, ambulances and aid vehicles entered Oleshky, a town in Ukraine's southern Kherson region that Russian forces have held since 2022, and began taking out residents who wished to leave[1,2]. Reuters described the operation as a joint relief effort by Russia and Ukraine, the first of its kind in four years and eight months of war[2]. The convoy was made up of 25 buses, nine ambulances and buses for bedridden people, and at least 50 Russian medical workers took part[3].

It took place under a local ceasefire that began at 03:00 Kyiv time on 27 October and is due to end at 22:59 on 1 November[4,5]. On the evening of 30 October, Ukraine's human rights ombudsman, Dmytro Lubinets, said the truce had lasted 87 hours without a recorded violation[5,6].

No confirmed total of evacuees was available on the day. Ukraine's prime minister, Serhii Koretskyi, gave a figure of 527 residents heading for Ukrainian-controlled territory and then withdrew it, saying he had shared it by mistake[3,7,8].

A town cut off and a disputed record of harm

Oleshky lies on the eastern bank of the Dnipro, opposite the Ukrainian-held city of Kherson[9]. The White House puts its pre-war population at roughly 24,000 and the number remaining at an estimated 1,700 to 2,000, mostly older people[10]. After the destruction of the Kakhovka Dam in 2023, about 90 percent of the town's territory was flooded[9,10]. According to a timeline compiled by Meduza, the last shop closed in January 2026 and food deliveries stopped in late May[11].

On 25 June the UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission in Ukraine (HRMMU) called for a local ceasefire, with its head, Danielle Bell, saying there was "a desperate need for a local ceasefire so that people can leave"[12]. The HRMMU recorded at least 29 civilians killed and 54 injured in Oleshky and neighbouring Hola Prystan in 2026, as of 25 June, based on interviews with more than 20 residents of frontline communities[12]. It said it "has not been able to conclusively establish responsibility in individual attacks"[12].

Human Rights Watch found that Ukrainian forces "may have used drones and mines on roads in and around Oleshky", while stating that it had not determined responsibility for specific attacks[13]. The Russian Defence Ministry said on 26 October that Ukraine had struck the town 18 times with artillery and carried out more than 120 drone attacks in the previous week; Reuters said it could not verify these claims independently[14]. Novaya Gazeta Europe reported that volunteers documented the first deaths from starvation over the summer, "though no one can officially confirm them"[15].

Competing accounts of how the truce was secured

On 16 October Pope Leo XIV telephoned President Vladimir Putin from a Rome hospital and, according to the Vatican, called for a ceasefire so that food and medical aid could get in[16]. The Kremlin said Putin supported a Vatican initiative to help deliver supplies[16]. The Vatican newspaper L'Osservatore Romano later credited the pope with the ceasefire on its front page[17].

On 23 October the White House announced that the first lady, Melania Trump, had secured the ceasefire, after learning of the town's situation during a private discussion at her Fostering the Future Together event during the 81st UN General Assembly[10,17]. She said that "without my intervention, the possibility to bring humanitarian aid into the city and to evacuate the elderly would be impossible"[17,18]. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov welcomed her "significant contribution"[19].

The truce covered only one area. As it took effect, Russia struck Kyiv overnight with ballistic missiles and damaged the Southern Bridge, which President Volodymyr Zelensky called "a serious escalation", Reuters reported[20].

A route through Russia and the question of consent

Ukrainian officials had expected residents choosing Ukraine to cross the Dnipro directly, but the Financial Times reported that these residents would first be taken to Russia[21]. On 30 October the buses reached Armiansk in occupied Crimea, where the convoy split, and those heading for Ukraine went on to Anapa in Russia's Krasnodar region, from where they were to fly to Belarus and continue by bus[8].

The detour has raised fears that evacuation could turn into deportation. Euronews recalled that in 2022 civilians fleeing Mariupol and other occupied areas were taken into Russian-controlled territory, where many went through compulsory security screening known as filtration[22]. The same report noted that passing through Russia is not in itself deportation; the test is whether people leave voluntarily and keep their freedom of movement[22]. Russia's human rights commissioner, Yana Lantratova, said that "forcible removal of families with children from Alyoshki is unacceptable", using the Russian name for the town[23].

Military suspicions, diplomacy and what remains uncertain

The Institute for the Study of War (ISW), a research group, assessed that "Russian forces may use the limited ceasefire to reconstitute their forces in Kherson Oblast"[24]. The diplomatic context helps explain why both governments agreed. Reuters' Moscow correspondent Andrew Osborn wrote that each side wants to show President Donald Trump that it is serious about peace and to avoid blame if problems arise[2]. The Moscow Times reported that the truce was agreed as Ukraine, Russia and the United States prepared technical talks in Abu Dhabi for the first half of November[25]. Kyrylo Budanov, the head of Zelensky's office, called it "an important operation, and it must be continued", while Peskov said such an arrangement "absolutely could" be repeated for humanitarian purposes[1]. Reuters reported, however, that he also described Oleshky as a specific and isolated case with no wider implications for the peace process for now[2].

Several matters remain uncertain. Neither government has confirmed how many people were evacuated or how many chose Russia over Ukraine[3,7]. Responsibility for individual drone strikes and mines has not been established, and Russian claims of Ukrainian strikes could not be independently verified[12,13,14]. Public statements alone cannot show how much the Vatican and the White House each contributed to the agreement.

1A joint convoy reaches a besieged town on the Dnipro

On Friday 30 October 2026, a convoy of buses, ambulances and aid vehicles entered Oleshky, a town in Ukraine's southern Kherson region that Russian forces have held since 2022, and began taking out residents who wished to leave[1,2]. Reuters described the operation as a joint relief effort by Russia and Ukraine, the first of its kind in four years and eight months of war[2]. According to agency reporting published by Al Jazeera, the convoy was made up of 25 buses, nine ambulances and buses for bedridden people, and at least 50 Russian medical workers took part[11]. Ukraine's human rights ombudsman, Dmytro Lubinets, said the buses had a combined capacity of 750 passengers, according to the Ukrainian state news agency Ukrinform[3].

The operation took place under a local ceasefire that began at 03:00 Kyiv time on 27 October and is due to end at 22:59 on 1 November[3,4]. On the evening of 30 October, Lubinets said the truce had lasted 87 hours without a recorded violation[4,15]. Residents had until 14:00 local time to leave, and the distribution of aid to those staying began after 14:30[4]. The supplies included food, medical goods, hygiene products and fuel for generators[11].

Speaking in the town, Russia's human rights commissioner, Yana Lantratova, said some residents were gathering to leave for Russia and others for Ukraine, adding that "everyone will be given the opportunity to do so"[1]. Lubinets said that evening that "those who wished to travel to Ukraine were able to leave Oleshky; they are currently on their way"[15]. No confirmed total was available on the day. Ukraine's prime minister, Serhii Koretskyi, gave a figure of 527 residents heading for Ukrainian-controlled territory and then withdrew it, saying he had shared it by mistake and that official numbers would follow once the evacuation was complete[9,11,12].

Officials on both sides presented the truce as significant. David Arakhamia, who leads President Volodymyr Zelensky's faction in parliament, called it the "biggest de-escalation since 2022"[16,17]. Kyrylo Budanov, the head of Zelensky's office, called it "an important operation, and it must be continued"[1]. The Kremlin spokesman, Dmitry Peskov, said such an arrangement "absolutely could" be repeated for humanitarian purposes[1]. Reuters reported, however, that he also described Oleshky as a specific and isolated case with no wider implications for the peace process for now[2].

2A riverside town cut off by occupation, flood, mines and drones

Oleshky lies on the eastern, or left, bank of the Dnipro, opposite the Ukrainian-held city of Kherson, and Russian forces captured it in the first days of the full-scale invasion they launched in February 2022[18]. The White House puts its pre-war population at roughly 24,000 and the number remaining at an estimated 1,700 to 2,000, mostly older people, along with about 50 children[7]. After the destruction of the Kakhovka Dam in 2023, about 90 percent of the town's territory was flooded, according to the same statement[7,18]. The Kyiv Independent reported that residents who lost documents in the flood later struggled to obtain the Russian passports needed to receive aid[19].

Conditions then worsened steadily. The Moscow Times reported that conditions had deteriorated since late 2025 and that roads around the town were heavily mined and badly damaged[20]. According to a timeline compiled by Meduza, the town's last shop closed in January 2026 and food deliveries stopped in late May[5]. Novaya Gazeta Europe dated the last delivery to 26 May. It also reported that on 2 June a mine exploded on the outskirts as four aid vehicles approached, injuring four drivers, one of whom died[21]. Residents described walking 23 kilometres to the market in Radensk, a journey of six to eight hours each way[21]. Tetiana Khasanenko, identified by Al Jazeera as the town's mayor, said that "most Oleshky residents eat once a day"[22].

International pressure grew through the summer and autumn. On 25 June the UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission in Ukraine (HRMMU) called for a local ceasefire, with its head, Danielle Bell, saying there was "a desperate need for a local ceasefire so that people can leave"[6]. In September, Ukraine's foreign minister, Andrii Sybiha, said he would press the issue at the UN General Assembly, and Bell said there had been no progress on an organised evacuation or a local ceasefire[23]. The Kremlin acknowledged at the time that the town faced "critical problems with food and medicine supplies" but said the Russian military was dealing with them[23]. The Council of Europe's human rights commissioner, Michael O'Flaherty, said he was "alarmed by the rapidly deteriorating humanitarian crisis in Oleshky"[24]. On 1 October the European Union told the OSCE Permanent Council that Russian forces had "prevented residents from leaving Oleshky" and that drone attacks and landmines had impeded evacuations[25].

3What the casualty and hunger figures can and cannot show

The most widely cited casualty figure comes from the HRMMU, which recorded at least 29 civilians killed and 54 injured in Oleshky and neighbouring Hola Prystan in 2026, as of 25 June[6]. The mission based its account on interviews with more than 20 residents of frontline communities in the occupied part of the region. It estimated that up to 6,000 civilians, including more than 180 children, remained in those communities[6]. It said the attacks took place in territory controlled by Russian forces that was under attack by short-range drones launched by Ukrainian forces, but that it "has not been able to conclusively establish responsibility in individual attacks"[6]. The OSCE's Office for Democratic Institutions and Human Rights (ODIHR) gave a similar estimate of about 6,000 residents in Oleshky and Hola Prystan as of May 2026. It cited accounts that Russian forces had stopped residents leaving and "used civilians to shield military positions"[26].

Human Rights Watch, which interviewed six residents who left between October 2025 and May 2026, reported that residents described passage through Russian checkpoints as depending on the discretion of Russian forces[13]. It found that Ukrainian forces "may have used drones and mines on roads in and around Oleshky", while stating that it had not determined responsibility for specific attacks[13]. In September 2025 the UN Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine reported that Russian forces were using short-range drones from the left bank to attack civilians on the Ukrainian-held right bank. In May 2025 it had concluded that such attacks in Kherson province amounted to crimes against humanity[27]. The commission said it had been unable to conclude its investigation into Russian allegations of Ukrainian drone attacks, citing lack of access[27].

Russian officials give a different account. The Russian Defence Ministry said on 26 October that Ukraine had struck the town 18 times with artillery and carried out more than 120 drone attacks in the previous week; Reuters said it could not verify these claims independently[14]. Vladimir Saldo, the Russian-installed governor, reported 68 people killed across the region in 2026[20]. Peskov has said Kyiv obstructs humanitarian deliveries. Lubinets, for his part, said Russia refused to open a green corridor and was using civilians "as a shield"[21,22].

Deaths from hunger are the least certain part of the record. Novaya Gazeta Europe reported that volunteers documented the first deaths from starvation over the summer, "though no one can officially confirm them"[21]. A recent escapee told the Moscow Times that people had been eating grass for two months. Ukraine dropped about four tonnes of food by drone on 4 October, although officials said Russian forces had confiscated some earlier supplies[20].

4Competing accounts of how the truce was secured

Two diplomatic channels came before the agreement. On 16 October Pope Leo XIV telephoned President Vladimir Putin from a Rome hospital where he was recovering from surgery. According to the Vatican, he expressed "deep concern about the grave humanitarian situation" in Oleshky and called for a ceasefire so that food and medical aid could get in[28]. The Kremlin said Putin supported a Vatican initiative to help deliver supplies, and called the conversation "constructive and conducted in a spirit of mutual respect"[28,29]. On 19 October the pope spoke to Zelensky and urged the two sides to co-operate[30]. The Vatican newspaper L'Osservatore Romano later credited the pope with the ceasefire on its front page. Al Jazeera reported that the papal envoy, Cardinal Matteo Zuppi, had pressed Russian authorities on Oleshky in Moscow in late September[8].

On 23 October the White House announced that the first lady, Melania Trump, had secured the ceasefire. It said she had learned of the town's situation during a private discussion at her Fostering the Future Together event during the 81st UN General Assembly[7,8]. NBC News reported that she drew on contacts made through her efforts to reunite Ukrainian children with their families[1]. She said that "without my intervention, the possibility to bring humanitarian aid into the city and to evacuate the elderly would be impossible"[8,16]. Peskov welcomed her "significant contribution" and said the matter had been discussed in a recent call between Putin and President Donald Trump[31].

The start date changed. The White House statement said the ceasefire would begin on 29 October[7]. On 23 October, Russia's chief of the General Staff, Valery Gerasimov, announced a halt to hostilities on the Kherson front from 04:00 on 27 October, and Ukraine's General Staff gave a start of 03:00 on 27 October, which LIGA.net reported as the same start time[32,33]. Allowing for the one-hour difference between Moscow and Kyiv, both times are 01:00 GMT, which is when Al Jazeera reported the Russian pause began[18]. Melania Trump later said the timing had been "accelerated two days ahead of schedule to protect the safety of civilians", and that the ceasefire area had been extended beyond Oleshky within the Kherson region[34].

The truce covered only one area. As it took effect, Russia struck Kyiv overnight with ballistic missiles and damaged the Southern Bridge, which Zelensky called "a serious escalation", Reuters reported[35]. Overnight on 28 October Russian forces launched 88 Shahed-type drones at Ukraine. The Kyiv Independent reported that Russian strikes had killed two people and injured 17 elsewhere in the country over the previous day[36].

5A route through Russia and Belarus and the question of consent

Ukrainian officials had expected residents choosing Ukraine to cross the Dnipro directly. Before the operation, however, the Financial Times reported, as cited by Meduza, that these residents would first be taken to Russia[17]. On 30 October the buses reached Armiansk in occupied Crimea, where the convoy split. Those heading for Ukraine went on to Anapa in Russia's Krasnodar region, from where they were to fly to Belarus and continue by bus[9]. Al Jazeera, citing agency reports, said they would enter Ukraine's Chernihiv region through a border crossing commonly used for prisoner exchanges[11]. Documents shown to Fox News and reported by Meduza said a Russian Defence Ministry aircraft would fly them from Anapa to Gomel in Belarus on 31 October[37]. The Associated Press put Anapa about 280 miles (450 kilometres) away[38].

The detour has raised fears that evacuation could turn into deportation. Euronews recalled that in 2022 civilians fleeing Mariupol and other occupied areas were taken into Russian-controlled territory, where many went through compulsory security screening known as filtration. It noted that Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International had documented forcible transfers as potential war crimes[10]. The same report noted that passing through Russia is not in itself deportation; the test is whether people leave voluntarily and keep their freedom of movement[10]. The Institute for the Study of War (ISW), a research group, questioned whether the process would be free of coercion[39].

Russian officials reject that framing. According to the Russian state news agency TASS, Lantratova said that "forcible removal of families with children from Alyoshki is unacceptable", using the Russian name for the town. She also said that "Alyoshki is Russian territory, where only Russian laws apply"[40]. Euronews noted that this claim is rejected internationally[10]. Rodion Miroshnik, an ambassador-at-large at the Russian foreign ministry, said slightly more than 1,000 people remained[40].

Other concerns rest on single sources. Kseniia Arkhipova, a volunteer who has helped evacuate residents, told the Kyiv Independent that Russia had broken the agreement by starting to move people before the agreed date[9]. Kyiv Post reported a warning from Ukraine's Security Service on 7 October that occupying servicemen had been ordered to prepare "compromising" interviews with residents[41]. In an opinion piece, Peter Pomerantsev and Aliide Naylor argued that residents must not become props in Russian propaganda[42]. Reuters reported that the International Committee of the Red Cross had not been asked to help but stood ready to do so[2].

6Military suspicions and the prospect of peace talks

Many people in Kherson doubt Russia's motives. Kyiv Post quoted a Ukrainian service member as saying that Ukrainian forces "do not attack because there are civilians there". It also quoted the journalist Serhiy Nikitenko as saying that Russia "uses them as cover, setting up positions directly inside their residential buildings"[43]. A Kherson businessman told the paper that earlier corridors had been used to bring in drone operators, generator fuel and ammunition[43]. These are allegations from residents and have not been independently checked.

The ISW assessed that "Russian forces may use the limited ceasefire to reconstitute their forces in Kherson Oblast". Kyiv Post noted that Russia's defence minister, Andrei Belousov, inspected the Dnepr grouping of forces on 27 October[39]. The German newspaper Bild, as reported by the Ukrainian outlet RBC-Ukraine, quoted Ukrainian soldiers who said lorries were moving Russian combat units and that Grad multiple rocket launchers had been deployed near Radensk. One soldier did not rule out an attempt to cross the river after the ceasefire ends[44]. RBC-Ukraine noted that no independent confirmation exists that an offensive is being prepared[44].

The diplomatic context helps explain why both governments agreed. Reuters' Moscow correspondent Andrew Osborn wrote that each side wants to show President Trump that it is serious about peace and to avoid blame if problems arise[2]. He wrote that Moscow seeks full control of the Donbas and Ukraine's exclusion from NATO, while Kyiv wants continued US intelligence sharing and weapons such as Patriot missiles[2]. The Moscow Times reported that the truce was agreed as Ukraine, Russia and the United States prepared technical talks in Abu Dhabi for the first half of November, after earlier talks stalled in February[45]. It added that the two sides had each released 270 servicemen in their first exchange since August[45].

It is not yet clear whether the arrangement will be repeated. The documents reported by Meduza referred to "further repatriation operations" from towns in the Kherson region at least once a month, with US coordination[37]. In June the UN humanitarian coordinator for Ukraine, Matthias Schmale, had argued that "a localized temporary ceasefire would be sufficient to deliver humanitarian assistance"[46].

Uncertainties and open questions

Several central facts remain unsettled. Neither government has confirmed how many people were evacuated, or how many chose Russia over Ukraine, since Koretskyi withdrew his figure of 527[11,12]. Estimates of the remaining population vary: a Russian diplomat gave slightly more than 1,000, Meduza about 1,500 and the White House 1,700 to 2,000[5,7,40]. Deaths from starvation reported by volunteers have not been officially confirmed[21].

Responsibility for the drone strikes and mines around the town has not been established. The HRMMU and Human Rights Watch both say they could not establish responsibility for individual attacks, and Reuters could not verify the Russian Defence Ministry's strike counts[6,13,14]. The HRMMU figure of 29 deaths is a stated minimum, and the mission's account draws on interviews with more than 20 residents[6]. Several other claims rest on a single source each: that Russia began moving residents early, that Russian forces are massing near the Dnipro, and Lantratova's statement that two people were killed during demining[4,9,44]. Public statements alone cannot show how much the Vatican and the White House each contributed to the agreement.

Conclusion

The operation shows that Russia and Ukraine could agree to a narrow humanitarian arrangement with outside mediation, even while strikes continued elsewhere[34,35,36]. Its immediate result is that a convoy reached a town that UN monitors had described as desperate, and that some residents left for Ukraine and others for Russia[4,6,11,15]. Its wider meaning depends on questions that remain open. These include how many people left and whether all did so freely, what happens to those who stayed once the truce expires on 1 November, and whether Russian forces use the pause to regroup, as the ISW has warned[3,39]. Officials in Kyiv and Moscow have both spoken of continuing or repeating the arrangement[1]. Neither the UN nor the Red Cross had been asked to take part in the operation[2]. Until independent monitors have direct access, much of the evidence about Oleshky will continue to come from the warring parties and from residents who have managed to leave.
