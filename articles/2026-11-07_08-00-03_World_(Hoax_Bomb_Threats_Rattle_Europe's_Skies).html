Five days of emailed threats against flights

Between Monday 2 November and Friday 6 November 2026, bomb threats against dozens of passenger flights forced diversions, fighter escorts and searches at airports across Europe, and no explosives were found on any aircraft[1,2,3]. The first case came on 2 November, when a Wizz Air flight from London to Sofia was diverted to Budapest with a Hungarian Gripen fighter as escort[4]. Rhys Jones, analysis output manager at Osprey Flight Solutions, told Reuters that seven confirmed threats against flights in a single day was exceptional[5].

The largest wave came on Thursday 5 November, and the totals differ depending on who did the counting. CBS News reported between 20 and 27 affected flights, Al Jazeera reported at least 27 passenger flights disrupted in more than ten countries, and CNN counted at least 14 flights affected[1,6,7]. Eurocontrol counted threats against 14 flights that day, about 0.05% of roughly 26,300 flights in European airspace, according to figures reported by Around Prague[8]. The reports do not explain how they counted.

On Friday 6 November at least 17 flights had been disrupted by midday GMT, according to Al Jazeera[3]. Prague's Václav Havel Airport alone reported nine threatened flights[8,9].

How an anonymous email diverts an airliner

Czech and Italian authorities confirmed that the threats arrived by anonymous email, and a representative of Prague's airport told CNN that the messages claimed bombs had been placed on flights[7]. Each threat was treated as potentially real. An Aer Lingus Airbus A320 flying from Dublin to Rome was diverted to the Évreux air base in northern France with 151 passengers and six crew on board[1,4,6]. At Murcia airport in Spain, operations were suspended for two hours while national police and TEDAX bomb disposal specialists checked a Ryanair aircraft carrying 114 passengers and seven crew[10].

Under European Union Aviation Safety Agency guidance, as summarised by the risk consultancy S-RM, an aircraft that receives a threat must land at the nearest suitable aerodrome to be investigated, and threats are judged by how credible and how specific they are[11]. Al Jazeera reported an expert estimate that each flight disruption costs an airline about $250,000, a single figure whose basis was not set out in detail[3].

Dublin newsrooms and competing claims of responsibility

RTÉ received an emailed threat at about 8.30am on Friday and evacuated its Dublin staff, according to the trade publication TVBEurope[12]. The same morning, identical emailed threats led to evacuations at Newstalk, Today FM, Virgin Media and the Irish Independent in Dublin, while Sky News in the UK also received threats[12,13,14]. The Irish government said it seemed likely that the media threats and the aviation threats formed "a co-ordinated campaign of disruption", but added: "It is not known yet who is responsible for these threats, whether it is individuals or a centralised or co-ordinated effort"[12]. The emails arrived six days before the European Political Community meeting in Dublin[14,15].

The most prominent claim of responsibility is an email signed by "Fighters of the Male State"[2,16]. LBC reported that Italian media had received an email attributed to the group, which said it would "turn the skies red" in protest at what it called a "feminist regime"[17]. Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty reports that Male State was founded in 2016 by Vladislav Pozdnyakov, a fitness coach, and that the group was banned in Russia in 2021[16,17]. Pozdnyakov and a linked Telegram channel have both denied involvement and said the group no longer exists[2,16].

A separate anonymous email on 2 November, claiming to come from Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, reached 271 Italian schools, and Italy's Postal Police opened an investigation without having verified the claimed affiliation or any link to the aviation threats[18]. With two very different claimed senders in a single week, neither signature can be accepted without forensic evidence about where the emails came from.

The case for a state-directed hybrid campaign

The argument that a state is behind the threats rests on context rather than on published evidence about these particular emails. In February 2026 the researchers Julian Lanchès and Kacper Rekawek identified 151 Russian-sponsored sabotage and terrorist incidents in Europe between February 2022 and February 2026, up from 110 in their previous report, using open sources and disclosures by security authorities[19]. They found that 95% of the 172 perpetrators were ordinary citizens with no formal tie to Russian intelligence[19]. In early October 2025, after drone intrusions disrupted airports ahead of a European leaders' summit in Copenhagen, the head of Danish Defence Intelligence, Thomas Ahrenkiel, said that "Russia is waging a hybrid war against Denmark because they want to create uncertainty", while Denmark's defence minister acknowledged that the operator had not been conclusively identified and Russia denied involvement[20].

The Taoiseach, Micheál Martin, said there was "a general view that all of the increases in hybrid attacks has a Russian connection", while also saying that "We don't know definitively" who was responsible[13]. Spain's foreign minister, José Manuel Albares, said: "The most reasonable assumption is that we are facing an attempt at hybrid aggression and a form of psychological warfare"[1,16].

Copycats and the limits of attribution

No government has formally attributed the threats. A European Commission spokesperson said that "It is too early to draw conclusions on whether the reported incidents are linked"[21]. The Dutch justice minister, David van Weel, said there was currently no evidence of state involvement, although no possibility had been ruled out[9]. Matthew Borie of Osprey Flight Solutions gave the Associated Press three possible explanations: copycat incidents set off by a single threat, multiple threats from one person or group, and hybrid warfare by state actors[22]. Hoax waves on this scale have happened elsewhere. India's Minister of State for Civil Aviation told the Lok Sabha in November 2024 that around 1,000 hoax bomb threat messages and calls had been received that year, compared with 122 in 2023, and no real threat was found[23].

Several basic facts remain unsettled. Counts for 5 November range from 14 flights in Eurocontrol's figures to at least 27 in Al Jazeera's tally[6,8]. Officials have said it is still unclear whether an individual or a co-ordinated effort lies behind the threats[7]. The Male State founder denies involvement, and suggestions that he has links to the FSB rest on speculation, fuelled partly by Pozdnyakov himself and by Russian rights activists[16]. Firmer conclusions depend on whether national investigators publish technical evidence on where the emails came from.

2How an anonymous email diverts an airliner

Czech and Italian authorities confirmed that the threats arrived by anonymous email, and a representative of Prague's airport told CNN that the messages claimed bombs had been placed on flights[17]. In Rome, the email of 3 November apparently reached a company referred to as ADR and reported "the presence of explosive devices" on flights arriving from Lisbon and Dublin, according to investigative sources quoted by L'Unione Sarda[19]. In Spain, three emailed threats arrived within 20 minutes on 5 November, naming an Air Europa departure from Madrid to Paris and two Iberia arrivals from Rome and Milan, although Al Jazeera reported two Air Europa departures and four Iberia arrivals[8,13].

The responses show that each threat was treated as potentially real. An Aer Lingus Airbus A320 flying from Dublin to Rome was diverted to the Évreux air base in northern France with 151 passengers and six crew on board[1,8,13]. Al Jazeera reported that, according to the office of the French prime minister, Sébastien Lecornu, fighter jets were sent to intercept the aircraft after the pilot "did not cooperate with authorities as expected"[8]. At Murcia airport in Spain, operations were suspended for two hours while national police and TEDAX bomb disposal specialists checked a Ryanair aircraft carrying 114 passengers and seven crew[20]. Warsaw-Modlin airport closed temporarily on 3 November, and Italian Eurofighters escorted Ryanair flight FR9611 into Rome[14]. On 5 November Rome's Leonardo da Vinci airport stopped flights for 30 minutes after a Finnair aircraft from Helsinki was escorted in[1].

Aviation security procedure explains why a hoax can cause so much disruption. Under European Union Aviation Safety Agency guidance, as summarised by the risk consultancy S-RM, an aircraft that receives a threat must land at the nearest suitable aerodrome to be investigated, and threats are judged by how credible and how specific they are[21]. Philip Baum, an aviation security specialist, told Al Jazeera that the incidents seemed coordinated to some extent, with authorities in several countries "evaluating the threat as being specific"[8]. France 24, a French state-owned broadcaster, reported that the threats were said to be "over and above the typical hoax in specificity"[2].

The threatened flights were a very small share of traffic, but each one can cost a great deal. Al Jazeera reported an expert estimate that each flight disruption costs an airline about $250,000, a single figure whose basis was not set out in detail[3]. David Kaminski-Morrow, air transport editor at FlightGlobal, told CBS News that "we currently live in a time of political aggression in regions including Europe, and it's a pretty effective tactic if someone is looking to cause disruption"[1].

3Dublin newsrooms evacuated days before a European summit

RTÉ received an emailed threat at about 8.30am on Friday and evacuated its Dublin staff, according to the trade publication TVBEurope[6]. The broadcaster first switched to pre-recorded programmes and later resumed live shows from its Cork offices[6]. The Irish Times reported that Morning Ireland on RTÉ Radio 1 was interrupted and that the presenter David McCullagh later broadcast from an outside facility, while Claire Byrne announced Newstalk's evacuation during her programme[4]. Gardaí, the Irish police, searched premises with sniffer dogs, and the Irish Times reported that Mediahuis staff had returned to their desks by mid-morning, whereas TVBEurope reported that staff remained barred from RTÉ's Dublin campus[4,6].

According to the Irish Times, the same email went to the Irish Independent's offices on Talbot Street, to Newstalk and to UK outlets including walesonline.co.uk and the Sunday Mirror[4]. TVBEurope reported that Bauer Media's Newstalk and Today FM and Virgin Media, at Ballymount in Dublin, were also targeted[6]. Sky News said: "The safety and security of our colleagues is our primary concern, and we are working closely with authorities to ensure our people and operations remain secure"[6]. Around Prague reported that Sky remained operational[7].

The Irish government's statement said: "It is not known yet who is responsible for these threats, whether it is individuals or a centralised or co-ordinated effort"[6]. Mr Martin said: "We will go after this to identify who's responsible and to hold them accountable", and the Tánaiste, Simon Harris, said: "The priority is that people are safe"[5]. The Minister for Justice, Jim O'Callaghan, said the threats were being taken extremely seriously in the context of hybrid attacks across Europe[4].

The emails arrived six days before the European Political Community meeting in Dublin[5,18]. The MEP Barry Andrews compared the episode with threats in Denmark before a summit there, saying that "the Danes were convinced a state actor was involved" and that it followed "a pattern of hybrid warfare, disinformation and other cyberattacks"[4]. In early October 2025, after drone intrusions disrupted airports ahead of a European leaders' summit in Copenhagen, the head of Danish Defence Intelligence, Thomas Ahrenkiel, said that "Russia is waging a hybrid war against Denmark because they want to create uncertainty", while Denmark's defence minister acknowledged that the operator had not been conclusively identified and Russia denied involvement[22]. In December 2025, drones flew into the flight path off Dublin Bay as President Volodymyr Zelenskiy's aircraft approached, and Mr O'Callaghan called this "part of a co-ordinated threat to EU and Ukrainian interests"[23].

4A banned Russian group's name and its founder's denial

The most prominent claim of responsibility is an email signed by "Fighters of the Male State"[2,9]. LBC reported that Italian media had received an email attributed to the group, which said it would "turn the skies red" in protest at what it called a "feminist regime" and named an Aer Lingus flight from Dublin to Rome and a Finnair flight from Helsinki to Rome[24]. Both flights were among those disrupted on 5 November[1]. CNN reported that an email sent to a logistics hub company working with Rome Fiumicino airport by a group calling itself "The Male State" falsely claimed that "explosive devices" were on specific flights[17].

Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty reports that Male State was founded in 2016 by Vladislav Pozdnyakov, a fitness coach. It says the group first campaigned against Russia's domestic violence legislation and then turned to threats and harassment of feminists[9]. The group was banned in Russia in 2021, and LBC reports that it was designated extremist in October 2021 and now operates as Male Legion[9,24]. According to CNN, the International Centre for Counter-Terrorism (ICCT) has described it as a misogynist movement that later became a vocal supporter of the war in Ukraine[17]. RFE/RL reports that its last confirmed activity was in 2022, when it targeted a bar in Tbilisi used by anti-Kremlin Russians[9].

Pozdnyakov's whereabouts are unknown. He runs a Telegram channel with about 5,400 subscribers that promotes Russia's war on Ukraine, and in October 2025 he faked his own death in Indonesia and reappeared two days later[9]. Russian authorities do not appear to have pursued him, which, together with hints from Pozdnyakov himself, has fed speculation that he has ties to the FSB, and some Russian rights activists have suggested that he began cooperating with the agency after his arrest[9]. Pozdnyakov and a linked Telegram channel have both denied involvement and said the group no longer exists[2,9].

The Male State signature is not the only identity attached to threats this month. On 2 November an anonymous email claiming to come from Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps reached 271 Italian schools, said that an explosive containing ammonium nitrate had been planted, and named Ryanair flight FR4438 from Riga to Rome[25]. A school complex in Rome's Tuscolano district evacuated about 1,800 pupils and staff, and Italy's Postal Police opened an investigation without having verified the claimed affiliation or any link to the aviation threats[25]. A name on a hoax email costs nothing to fake. With two very different claimed senders in a single week, neither signature can be accepted without forensic evidence about where the emails came from.

5The argument for a state-directed hybrid campaign

The argument that a state is behind the threats rests on context rather than on published evidence about these particular emails. On 13 October 2026 Ken McCallum, director general of the UK's MI5 spy agency, said that "The Russian military and intelligence services are once again escalating their attempts at hybrid warfare". Euronews reported that he described this activity as staying below the threshold of NATO's Article 5[26]. He said Iran was "keen on subcontracting its UK violence" to criminal proxies, and that Iran-linked activity had led to more than 50 arrests in the previous year[26].

The most systematic open-source count comes from the ICCT. In February 2026 the researchers Julian Lanchès and Kacper Rekawek identified 151 Russian-sponsored sabotage and terrorist incidents in Europe between February 2022 and February 2026, up from 110 in their previous report, using open sources and disclosures by security authorities[10]. Poland had 31 incidents, France 20, Lithuania and Germany 15 each, the UK 12 and Estonia 11, and the authors concluded that "support for Ukraine is the single most important factor shaping target selection"[10]. They found that 95% of the 172 perpetrators were ordinary citizens with no formal tie to Russian intelligence, and that "Financial gain is the single most important motivation for involvement in Russian kinetic activities"[10].

Aviation has been a target before. In January 2025 the Polish prime minister, Donald Tusk, accused Russia of planning "acts of terrorism", and Western security officials suggested that parcel explosions at European logistics sites were a test run for putting devices on cargo flights to the United States. The Kremlin spokesman, Dmitry Peskov, called these "absolutely unsubstantiated allegations against Russia"[27]. On 13 July 2026 the UK sanctioned an Iran-linked group known as HAYI, and Foreign Secretary Yvette Cooper said that "sitting behind IMCR were members of Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps Qods Force, who almost certainly directed IMCR attacks across Europe"[28]. Al Jazeera reported that Moscow and Tehran both deny waging such campaigns[14].

Earlier the same week, on 2 and 3 November, hackers raised temperatures in internet-connected supermarket refrigeration systems in several countries, affecting stores including Musgrave's SuperValu shops in Ireland[29]. The EU commissioner Christophe Hansen called that attack "a deliberate attempt to disrupt our food supply chain"[30]. Mr Martin said he believed that the threats to flights and media organisations and the cyberattack on supermarket refrigeration systems were "co-ordinated"[4]. Spain's foreign minister, José Manuel Albares, said: "The most reasonable assumption is that we are facing an attempt at hybrid aggression and a form of psychological warfare"[1,9]. Paul Mason, an air transport security expert, told CBS News that the threats appeared to have been made credibly across many airlines, which "would suggest to me that it's been a very deliberate act"[1].

6Copycats, opportunists and the limits of attribution

No government has formally attributed the threats. In remarks reported on 4 November, a European Commission spokesperson said that "It is too early to draw conclusions on whether the reported incidents are linked" and that the European Union Aviation Safety Agency was involved, according to CGTN, China's state broadcaster[11]. Later in the week a European commissioner said it was too early to say who was behind the threats, Al Jazeera reported[3]. The Dutch justice minister, David van Weel, said there was currently no evidence of state involvement, although no possibility had been ruled out, according to Xinhua, the Chinese state news agency[12].

Matthew Borie of Osprey Flight Solutions gave the Associated Press three possible explanations. The first was copycat incidents set off by a single threat, the second was multiple threats from one person or group, and the third was hybrid warfare by state actors. He added that "Iranian hybrid warfare is just as active in certain pockets of Europe as well"[15]. The UK transport secretary, Heidi Alexander, called the threats "malicious hoaxes" and said: "we have established processes for dealing with hoax and malicious threats"[24]. Jones of Osprey argued that "The lack of clear target state, airline or route indicates that general disruption to aviation operations is the aim", which describes the effect but does not identify the sender[14].

Hoax waves on this scale have happened elsewhere. India's Minister of State for Civil Aviation, Murlidhar Mohol, told the Lok Sabha in November 2024 that around 1,000 hoax bomb threat messages and calls had been received that year, compared with 122 in 2023. More than 250 first information reports (formal police complaints) had been filed and 12 people arrested, and no real threat was found. These figures were reported by News On AIR, India's state broadcaster[31]. That episode shows that hoaxing can grow into hundreds of incidents through imitation and low cost. It does not show what caused the European wave.

The link to parallel incidents is also disputed. Romania's cybersecurity director called the refrigeration attack "semi-amateurish" and ruled out state involvement[30]. Paul Reuter, vice-president of the European Cockpit Association, said that using civil aviation for hoaxes or geopolitical disruption was "totally unacceptable"[7]. Who is responsible affects the response. A state campaign would call for diplomatic and security measures, whereas copycat or criminal hoaxing is mostly a matter for police and prosecutors.

Uncertainties and open questions

Several basic facts are still unsettled. Counts for 5 November range from 14 flights in Eurocontrol's figures to at least 27 in Al Jazeera's tally[7,8]. Officials have said it is still unclear whether an individual or a co-ordinated effort lies behind the threats[17]. The Italian Postal Police had not verified the Revolutionary Guard identity claimed in the threats to schools[25]. The Male State lead rests on a reported email signature, "Fighters of the Male State". The group's founder denies involvement, and suggestions that he has FSB links rest on speculation, fuelled partly by Pozdnyakov himself and by Russian rights activists[9]. The detail that the Aer Lingus pilot did not cooperate as expected rests on a statement from the office of the French prime minister, as reported by Al Jazeera[8]. The ICCT dataset behind much of the argument about Russian hybrid activity relies on open sources and official disclosures, and the incidents it records are acts of sabotage and terrorism[10]. The estimate of about $250,000 per disrupted flight is one reported figure without a published method[3]. Accounts of the return to Dublin newsrooms also vary: the Irish Times reported that Mediahuis staff were back at their desks by mid-morning, while TVBEurope reported that staff were still barred from RTÉ's campus[4,6]. The Taoiseach's reference to a Russian connection was a general view, not an investigative finding[4].

Conclusion

The established record is limited but solid. Over five days, emailed threats disrupted dozens of flights in many countries and then reached newsrooms in Ireland and the UK, and no device was found[1,3,4,17]. Who sent them has not been established. The Irish government describes a likely co-ordinated campaign[6]. Spain's foreign minister speaks of hybrid aggression, and Ireland's Taoiseach refers to a general view that there is a Russian connection[4,9]. The European Commission says it is too early to link the incidents, and the Dutch justice minister says there is no evidence of state involvement[11,12]. The group whose name was on the emails has disowned them[9]. Russian-sponsored sabotage in Europe is well documented, yet clear attribution of such incidents remains challenging because perpetrators are rarely identified[10]. The ninth meeting of the European Political Community is due to take place in Dublin on 12 November[18]. Firmer conclusions depend on whether national investigators publish technical evidence on where the emails came from.
