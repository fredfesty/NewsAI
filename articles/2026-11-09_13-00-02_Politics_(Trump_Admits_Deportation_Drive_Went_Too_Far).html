A rare admission about the deportation drive

President Donald Trump said his administration's mass deportation campaign had been too harsh and had cost him politically, in an interview with the CBS programme 60 Minutes broadcast on Sunday 8 November 2026[1,2]. Asked about the policy by the correspondent Ross Douthat, he replied: "I think it might be too tough, yeah," and added: "That hurt me"[3]. According to CBS, the interview took place three days after Republicans suffered heavy midterm losses, particularly in South Texas and other Latino-majority areas[2].

Trump split his record in two. "I get an A+ for closing up the border. I get very high marks for getting the bad ones out, but I get hurt because we also took people that had good jobs," he said[4].

Asked whether he could agree with the incoming Democratic majorities on a legal status for undocumented workers, he said he could[5]. "The deal would be we would work something out," he said, and "I think that's something that's actually doable"[6,7].

On Friday 6 November, Trump named Stephen Miller, the deputy chief of staff and architect of the deportation programme, as White House chief of staff from Monday 9 November, replacing Susie Wiles[8,9]. The 60 Minutes transcript notes that the change was announced about an hour after the interview was recorded[3].

A heavy defeat and rival explanations for it

Democrats took control of both chambers of Congress in the midterm elections of Tuesday 3 November[10]. A guide to the new Congress published by the law firm K&L Gates counted at least 54 senators in the Democratic caucus, including independents, and Democratic gains of at least 33 House seats[11].

CBS News reported that 58% of voters said the administration's immigration actions had gone too far, although voters still trusted Republicans slightly more than Democrats on the issue, by 43% to 41%[2].

Trump offered his own explanations. "I was not on the ballot," he told Douthat, adding: "They didn't like the war. They didn't like the fact that gasoline went up"[3]. The US Energy Information Administration recorded an average retail price for regular gasoline of $4.153 per gallon on 2 November 2026, $1.134 higher than a year earlier[12].

Vice President JD Vance put it more bluntly. "The reason we lost is the war. The president himself said so last night," he wrote on X[13]. The AP VoteCast survey of more than 100,000 voters found that about 60% thought military action against Iran was the wrong decision. But only about a third put the war in the highest category of importance for their vote, against roughly half for the cost of living or government corruption[14]. Asked about Vance's post, Trump said: "I don't disagree with it"[3].

Funding, targets and contested numbers

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which became law on 4 July 2025, provides more than $75 billion over four years for Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), including $45 billion for detention and $29.9 billion for enforcement and removal operations[15].

The Department of Homeland Security (DHS) said in January 2026 that about 3 million people living in the country illegally had left during Trump's first year back in office. That figure included more than 675,000 deportations and an estimated 2.2 million self-deportations[16]. In September it said ICE had made 50,925 arrests in August 2026, the highest monthly figure on record[17].

The researcher Austin Kocher, using ICE detention data up to 4 April 2026, counted 234,236 removals in fiscal year 2026 up to that date, 74% more than at the same point in fiscal year 2025[18]. He cited a Cato Institute analysis finding that 73% of people in ICE custody had no criminal conviction and 5% had a violent conviction[18]. ICE warns that its figures for the current year may change until they are locked at the end of the fiscal year[19].

CBS News reported on 8 November that ICE officials had been told to make about 19,000 arrests a week, roughly 2,700 a day, up from an earlier target of 2,000 a day[20]. In October the Homeland Security Secretary, Markwayne Mullin, said: "The American people voted for mass deportations, and that's exactly what we're giving them"[21].

Reactions, public opinion and the Iran war

CBS News reported that Wiles submitted her resignation on Thursday night because of the midterm defeats[8]. Democrats see the appointment as the clearer signal. The Senate Democratic leader, Chuck Schumer, said: "Stephen Miller is the architect of the most barbaric, inhumane policies of this administration"[8]. The White House spokeswoman Lauren Bis told CBS News: "This administration will not slow down its efforts to arrest and deport criminal illegal aliens"[20].

Gallup surveyed 1,001 adults by telephone from 1 to 15 June 2026. In that survey, 73% said immigration was a good thing for the country, 58% opposed deporting all immigrants living in the country illegally, and 75% supported a path to citizenship[22].

The interview also covered the war with Iran. The United States and Israel launched a major attack on Iran on 28 February 2026[23]. Trump said: "I think, historically, this will be a very good decision," before adding: "Is it a good decision for today and tomorrow? Maybe not"[24]. Asked whether he would escalate, he said: "Well, I'm not looking to. I don't think there's any reason to"[24].

Open questions about enforcement and evidence

Asked whether the midterm results would bring a pullback in enforcement, a source quoted by CBS News said: "I would count on the opposite"[20]. Trump did not set out what legal status he would accept for undocumented workers[4]. Deportation totals depend on definitions: the DHS figure adds formal removals to an estimate of self-deportations, while independent counts based on ICE data use narrower categories[16,18]. Estimates of Iranian deaths differ by thousands depending on the source[25].

Asked about the policy by the correspondent Ross Douthat, he replied: "I think it might be too tough, yeah," and added: "That hurt me"[2]. In a version of the answer reported by ABC News, he said: "I think it was too tough. And I think -- I think ultimately, that hurt me"[12]. He added that a group "on the pretty far right" would "like everybody taken out"[14]. Asked whether he could agree with the incoming Democratic majorities on a legal status for undocumented workers, he said he could[4]. The remarks drew attention because of a decision taken before the broadcast. On Friday 6 November, Trump named Stephen Miller, the deputy chief of staff and architect of the deportation programme, as White House chief of staff from Monday 9 November, replacing Susie Wiles[5,6]. The 60 Minutes transcript notes that the change was announced about an hour after the interview was recorded[2]. The president's words and his choice of staff therefore pointed in different directions in the same week.

2A heavy midterm defeat and rival explanations for it

3The money, targets and numbers behind mass deportation

Congress paid for the deportation programme through the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which became law on 4 July 2025. A summary of the law by the immigration policy group Forum Together says it provides more than $75 billion over four years for Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), including $45 billion for detention and $29.9 billion for enforcement and removal operations, available until 30 September 2029[22]. While the bill was before Congress, the White House said it would pay for at least one million removals a year, at least 10,000 new ICE agents and an average daily detention population of at least 100,000 people[23].

The Department of Homeland Security (DHS) presents the results as records. In January 2026 it said that about 3 million people living in the country illegally had left during Trump's first year back in office. That figure included more than 675,000 deportations and an estimated 2.2 million self-deportations[24]. In September the department said ICE had made 50,925 arrests in August 2026, the highest monthly figure on record, after 50,208 in July[25]. The conservative outlet The Daily Wire, citing DHS, reported more than 1.25 million arrests and nearly 1.2 million deportations since Trump returned to office[14]. The self-deportation number is an estimate, and the January release does not say how it was calculated.

Independent analysts work from ICE's own published statistics, which give a narrower picture. The researcher Austin Kocher used ICE detention data up to 4 April 2026. He counted 234,236 removals in fiscal year 2026 up to that date, 74% more than at the same point in fiscal year 2025, and a record detained population of 70,766 on 24 January 2026[10]. He also counted 47 deaths in ICE custody since January 2025. He cited a Cato Institute analysis finding that 73% of people in ICE custody had no criminal conviction and 5% had a violent conviction[10]. Kocher notes that his counts include returns and expulsions handled by ICE but leave out Border Patrol expedited removals. ICE warns that its figures for the current year may change until they are locked at the end of the fiscal year[10,11].

There is no sign yet that the pace will slow. CBS News reported on 8 November that ICE officials had been told to make about 19,000 arrests a week, roughly 2,700 a day, up from an earlier target of 2,000 a day[7]. In October the Homeland Security Secretary, Markwayne Mullin, said: "The American people voted for mass deportations, and that's exactly what we're giving them"[26].

4Minneapolis killings and an earlier wobble over workers

The human cost of enforcement became a national issue in Minneapolis in January 2026. Renee Good, a US citizen, was killed when a federal agent fired into her vehicle during an ICE raid in the city[27]. On 24 January, Alex Pretti was shot dead by federal Border Patrol agents during an immigration enforcement operation. He was a 37-year-old US citizen who worked as an intensive care nurse at the Veterans Affairs medical centre[27]. CBS News describes the two as American citizens fatally shot by federal immigration officials during the January crackdown in Minneapolis, while Al Jazeera says they were killed during protests against the operations[7,13].

Official and eyewitness accounts of Pretti's death conflict. Kristi Noem, then Homeland Security Secretary, said: "He wasn't there to peacefully protest. He was there to perpetuate violence." The Department of Homeland Security said a Border Patrol agent had fired in self-defence after Pretti approached with a handgun and violently resisted attempts to disarm him[27]. Al Jazeera reported that bystander footage verified by Reuters showed him holding a mobile phone. Video appeared to show an agent removing a gun from him while he was pinned down, before an officer fired four shots at his back in rapid succession, followed by further shots. Two witnesses stated in sworn court filings that he did not brandish a gun[27]. France 24 associated Miller with scenes of masked immigration agents committing violence, noting that he had backed the federal agents who shot and killed two Americans earlier in the year[28].

The November remarks are not the first time Trump has questioned what enforcement does to the workforce. On 12 June 2025 he wrote on Truth Social: "Our great Farmers and people in the Hotel and Leisure business have been stating that our very aggressive policy on immigration is taking very good, long time workers away"[29]. The New York Times reported that ICE was then told to pause worksite enforcement at farms, meatpacking plants, restaurants and hotels[30]. It reported that Miller had urged a hard-line approach and that top White House officials were "furious" with the Agriculture Secretary, Brooke Rollins. It also reported, citing advocates, that farmworkers in Oxnard, California, were rounded up the day after the guidance was issued[30].

The 2025 episode shows that Trump has voiced sympathy for long-settled workers before, while his staff kept up the pace of enforcement. Critics and supporters are now asking whether the November remarks will go any further than that earlier wobble.

5Stephen Miller's promotion and the public mood

CBS News reported that Wiles submitted her resignation on Thursday night because of the midterm defeats. She had planned to stay through the lame-duck session of Congress[5]. Trump called Miller "one of my most trusted advisors", "very good politically" and a "great manager"[5]. Wiles, who ran Trump's 2024 campaign, was his longest-serving chief of staff; he had four in his first term[5,6].

Democrats see the appointment as the clearer signal. The Senate Democratic leader, Chuck Schumer, said: "Stephen Miller is the architect of the most barbaric, inhumane policies of this administration"[5]. After the interview aired, Schumer wrote: "Trump says his immigration crackdown went too far"[13,15]. The Associated Press, as published by PBS, quoted him as going on: "Apparently, his solution was to put the man responsible for it in charge of the White House"[15]. Al Jazeera quoted the line: "That's not a course correction. That's a promotion"[13]. Neither outlet explains the difference between the two versions.

The administration's formal position has not changed. The White House spokeswoman Lauren Bis told CBS News: "This administration will not slow down its efforts to arrest and deport criminal illegal aliens"[7]. One official, asked whether enforcement would ease after the election, said: "I would count on the opposite"[7]. Theresa Cardinal Brown, an immigration policy expert quoted by CBS, said: "They're going to continue apace. They have the money. They have the resources"[7].

Public opinion helps explain why Trump might want to soften his tone. Gallup surveyed 1,001 adults by telephone from 1 to 15 June 2026, with a margin of error of plus or minus four percentage points. In that survey, 73% said immigration was a good thing for the country, down from 79% a year earlier[8]. Among Republicans the positive view fell 14 points to 50%[8]. Some 58% opposed deporting all immigrants living in the country illegally, and 75% supported a path to citizenship for undocumented immigrants[8]. Taken together, these results describe a public that backs border control but resists blanket removals, the same division Trump described on air.

6The Iran war and the president's long view

The interview also covered the war with Iran, which Vance blamed for the Republican defeat[9]. The United States and Israel launched a major attack on Iran on 28 February 2026, and Trump announced that the Supreme Leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, had been killed[31]. Trump said Iran had "continued to develop its nuclear program", even though he had earlier said strikes in June 2025 had "obliterated" it[31]. Iran's Foreign Minister, Abbas Araghchi, called the attack "unprovoked, illegal and absolutely illegitimate". Iran said it had not enriched uranium since June 2025 but blocked international inspectors from the bombed sites[31].

The Wikipedia article on the conflict, which compiles reports from many sources, gives this sequence. A ceasefire on 8 April was followed by a memorandum signed on 17 June, which collapsed on 8 July after attacks on commercial ships, and hostilities resumed from September[32]. The same compilation lists death tolls that vary widely. Iranian reports give 3,528 killed, the rights group HRANA 3,684 and the World Health Organization at least 3,375, while US and Israeli estimates put Iranian military deaths alone at more than 6,000; 23 US service members and three contractors have been killed[32]. These figures come from parties with different interests and methods, and no independent count exists to settle them.

On 60 Minutes, Trump defended the war as a long-term judgement. "I think, historically, this will be a very good decision," he said, before adding: "Is it a good decision for today and tomorrow? Maybe not"[33]. He argued that "You can't let Iran have a nuclear weapon" and said Iran's nuclear material was "buried under thousands of tons of rock"[2]. Asked whether he would escalate, he said: "Well, I'm not looking to. I don't think there's any reason to"[33]. Iran's government rejects the US case for the war. In March, Araghchi told CBS's Face the Nation that "There are people being killed only because President Trump wants to have fun". He said about 440 kilograms of enriched uranium lay buried under rubble[34].

Diplomacy continues alongside the fighting. CBS News reported that on Monday 9 November Trump was due to meet Qatar's Prime Minister, Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani, who mediates indirect talks between Washington and Tehran[33]. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the talks were not "at the verge of a breakthrough"[33].

Uncertainties and open questions

Several important points are still open. On enforcement, a source quoted by CBS News expects the opposite of a pullback, and ICE's arrest targets have gone up[7]. Trump did not set out what legal status he would accept for undocumented workers, or say which Democrats had contacted him[12]. The seat counts used here were provisional, and two Senate races were still uncalled when they were published[17].

The polling evidence comes from surveys with different designs. CBS reported that 58% of voters said enforcement had gone too far, while The Daily Wire attributed a 58% figure to a Fox News poll[3,14]. The Texas turnout figures come from an advocacy organisation, not official returns[18]. Deportation totals depend on definitions. The DHS figure of about 3 million departures adds formal removals to an estimate of self-deportations. Independent counts based on ICE data use narrower categories, and ICE says its figures may be revised[10,11,24]. Federal officials and witnesses give conflicting accounts of Alex Pretti's death[27]. Estimates of Iranian deaths differ by thousands depending on the source[32].

Conclusion

Trump's statement that his deportation policy "might be too tough" admitted a political cost; it did not announce a new policy[2]. He paired it with an offer of a deal with Democrats on the status of some workers, while defending his record at the border and the removal of criminals[12,13]. In the same week he appointed the programme's architect as chief of staff, and his administration says enforcement will not slow[5,7]. On Iran, he accepted short-term damage but said history would prove him right[33].

The tests that matter are concrete. They are whether ICE's arrest targets fall, whether the new Democratic Congress and the White House produce any immigration bill, and whether the talks mediated by Qatar make progress. Until one of these moves, the evidence supports reading the interview as a change of tone that has not yet become a change of course.
