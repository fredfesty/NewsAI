First three-way talks in nine months open in Abu Dhabi

Delegations from the United States, Ukraine and Russia met in Abu Dhabi on 10 November 2026 for their first three-way negotiating session in about nine months[1,2]. The talks were scheduled to last two days, and a second session took place on 11 November[1,3]. On the first day the negotiators met for more than four hours. The subjects reportedly discussed included security guarantees for both sides, post-war reconstruction, a new security architecture and exchanges of prisoners of war[4]. No ceasefire, territorial arrangement or signed document was announced after the first day, and Foreign Policy reported that a significant breakthrough remained out of reach[1,2].

The United States was represented by the special envoy Steve Witkoff and by Jared Kushner, President Donald Trump's son-in-law[1,5]. According to TASS, Russia's delegation was led by the presidential aide Vladimir Medinsky[6]. TASS, citing Ukrainian media, reported that Ukraine's team was led by Rustem Umerov, head of the Foreign Intelligence Service[6]. European Pravda reported that representatives of the European Commission, the European Council, Germany, France and the United Kingdom at security-adviser level, together with the NATO secretary general's chief of staff, would attend, and that Ukraine's team would hold separate coordination meetings with European partners[7].

Kushner told the delegations that the aim was to create "a new framework" of security between Ukraine, Russia and Europe. He also said that the United States had "narrowed the gaps" between the two sides, but gave no details[3,5]. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, who did not attend, called the talks "very productive"[4,5]. He also warned: "It's not like something that's on the verge of being solved in the next 24 hours"[2]. President Volodymyr Zelensky, who was not in Abu Dhabi, described the first day as "constructive and quite substantive"[2,4,8]. The Kremlin spokesperson, Dmitry Peskov, said of the proposed security architecture: "We will not discuss any models publicly"[4].

From invasion to a stalled peace process

Russia launched what the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, Volker Türk, calls a "full-scale invasion" on 24 February 2022. Since then, UN monitors have verified at least 16,431 civilians killed, including 803 children, and 48,613 injured, according to figures published in July 2026[9,10]. In September 2022 Russia declared four Ukrainian regions, Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia and Kherson, to be its own territory, although it has never fully captured them[1]. On 12 October 2022 the UN General Assembly voted 143 to 5, with 35 abstentions, for a resolution that demanded that Russia reverse its annexation declaration[11].

Russia's demands about NATO are older than the invasion. In December 2021 Moscow published draft agreements that would have committed the alliance to "no further enlargement of the alliance, including in particular to Ukraine"[12]. A 28-point US plan, reported in November 2025, was drafted by Witkoff with input from Rubio and Kushner[13]. Three-way meetings in Abu Dhabi on 23–24 January and 4–5 February 2026 led to an exchange of 314 prisoners, 157 from each side, but produced no ceasefire or settlement[14].

The process then stalled. In March 2026 Peskov said the talks were on a "situational pause" because of the war involving Iran[15]. On 21 October Witkoff met Putin in St Petersburg for three hours, and Putin's adviser Yuri Ushakov said Putin had agreed "in principle" to resume three-way talks after 10 November[16].

Washington widens the agenda to European security

ABC News reported that the administration has shifted the focus of the talks from territory alone towards NATO and European security arrangements, and that key European countries were taking part in Abu Dhabi for the first time[17]. On 5 November Kushner announced a framework with five parts: permanent security consultations, crisis communication channels, greater military transparency, reciprocal measures to reduce the risk of escalation, and long-term arrangements for European security[18,19]. RFE/RL reported that the proposal included "no state having a veto over another's security choices or alliances"[18].

The Washington Post columnist David Ignatius reported, as relayed by Ukrainska Pravda, that "Kushner's model is the NATO-Russia Council"[20]. That council was created by the Rome Declaration of 28 May 2002[21]. NATO suspended practical civilian and military cooperation with Russia in April 2014 because of Russia's intervention in Ukraine and its annexation of Crimea[22].

AFP cited Russian sources as saying that settling the war was "merely one item on the agenda" and that Moscow's main objective was a review of the continent's whole security architecture[5]. Kyiv and its European partners object to the order of events. Zelensky told CNN that "The idea of discussing a European-Russian security infrastructure is not simple, and it can be dangerous", and warned against a "Yalta 2.0"[5,19]. A senior official from a close US ally said: "A Ukraine peace agreement has to be settled before an agreement on security architecture"[19]. Samuel Charap of the RAND Corporation told ABC News: "We're not going to have a new European security architecture agreement simultaneous to a ceasefire"[17].

Ceasefire first or settlement first

The sharpest disagreement is about what comes first. In remarks reported on 3 November, Lavrov rejected Ukrainian proposals for an immediate ceasefire and for partial truces, saying: "We insist not on a temporary ceasefire...but on reliably eliminating the root causes"[23]. In comments reported as the talks opened, he dismissed European calls for a ceasefire as an attempt to "buy time" for Kyiv[1,2]. CBS News said Moscow wants all four regions it declared annexed in 2022, while AFP reported a demand that Ukraine give up the parts of Donetsk it still controls[1,5]. PBS NewsHour reported that Russia wants Ukraine to abandon its hopes of joining NATO and to accept limits on its military, and that it insists on security guarantees for Russia itself[24].

Ukraine refuses to give up territory[2]. Zelensky said: "No one believes that Putin will stop if we give up the Donbas. He might pause — but then he will return in a year, two years or five years"[1]. AFP reported that Ukrainian officials want at least ten years of financial support to maintain an army of 800,000[5]. The European Council's conclusions of 16 October call on Russia to "agree to a full, unconditional and immediate ceasefire" and state that "Borders must not be changed by force"[25]. PBS NewsHour reported that Trump has said Moscow and Kyiv are "very close" to a deal[24].

Violence, competing readings and what remains uncertain

The fighting continues alongside the talks. UN monitors verified at least 328 civilians killed and 2,105 injured in September 2026, 85% more than in September 2025[26,27]. On 2 November glide bombs killed at least 31 civilians who had gathered for aid at a school in Komyshuvakha[28]. Alexander Drozdenko, governor of the Leningrad region, said 113 drones were shot down on the night of 9 November, and the Kirishi refinery caught fire[29]. On 16 October Putin claimed that Ukraine held only about 10% of the Donetsk region, whereas DeepState data show Russia occupying about 80%[30].

Those who see an opening point to pressure on both sides. Foreign Policy reported that the long war has "strained Russia's wartime economy"[2]. Sceptics see continuity. ABC News reported that Putin's core demands have not changed[17], and before the talks Lavrov said: "Our position has not changed. It has never changed since the beginning of the special military operation"[6]. European officials have said they would agree to a security framework with Russia only after a ceasefire, while Kushner favours running both processes at once[19,20].

Much of what happened in the negotiating room is not public. Meduza reported that Russia asked for the discussions to stay confidential[4]. Kushner's statement that the gaps had narrowed came without specifics[3,5]. Reports of Russia's territorial demand do not agree on whether it covers all four regions or only the rest of Donetsk[1,5]. Reported counts of drones downed over the Leningrad region on 11 November differ, at 92 and 146, both attributed to the regional governor[3,8]. The result of the second day, and whether a further round will follow, were not known when the talks resumed.

2From full-scale invasion to a stalled peace process

Russia launched what the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, Volker Türk, calls a "full-scale invasion" on 24 February 2022. Since then, UN monitors have verified at least 16,431 civilians killed, including 803 children, and 48,613 injured, according to figures published in July 2026[17,18]. Moscow calls its campaign a "special military operation". Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov used that term before the talks when he said Russia's position had never changed[15]. In September 2022 Russia declared four Ukrainian regions, Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia and Kherson, to be its own territory, although it has never fully captured them[1]. On 12 October 2022 the UN General Assembly voted 143 to 5, with 35 abstentions, for a resolution that described these regions as temporarily occupied by Russia and demanded that Russia reverse its annexation declaration[19]. Article 2(4) of the UN Charter requires member states to refrain from "the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state"[20].

Russia's demands about NATO are older than the invasion. In December 2021 Moscow published draft agreements that would have committed the alliance to "no further enlargement of the alliance, including in particular to Ukraine". The drafts would also have barred NATO forces from countries that joined after May 1997[13]. Steven Pifer of the Brookings Institution argued at the time that the drafts' publication, their unacceptable provisions and "the peremptory terms used by Russian officials" suggested "the Kremlin may want rejection"[13]. AFP reported that Ryabkov, now a member of the Abu Dhabi delegation, had previously presented Russian demands for NATO to withdraw from Eastern Europe[9].

A 28-point US plan, reported in November 2025, was drafted by Witkoff with input from Rubio and Kushner. It proposed capping Ukraine's armed forces at 600,000, recognising Crimea, Luhansk and Donetsk as de facto Russian, and freezing Kherson and Zaporizhzhia along the line of contact[21]. Three-way meetings in Abu Dhabi on 23–24 January and 4–5 February 2026 led to an exchange of 314 prisoners, 157 from each side, but produced no ceasefire or settlement[22]. TASS dates the last three-way meeting before November to 18 February, in Geneva[15].

The process then stalled. In March 2026 Peskov said the talks were on a "situational pause" because of the war involving Iran. Zelensky replied: "There has been a pause in the talks. It is time to end that"[23]. On 21 October Witkoff met Putin in St Petersburg for three hours. Afterwards Putin's adviser Yuri Ushakov said Putin had agreed "in principle" to resume three-way talks after 10 November[24]. At the start of November Witkoff announced that the round would cover "post-war security agreements, de-escalation, humanitarian measures, and ending the war"[25]. The National, an Abu Dhabi newspaper, noted that the talks came after the Republican Party's defeat in the US midterm elections[26].

3Washington widens the agenda to European security

ABC News reported that the administration has shifted the focus of the talks from territory alone towards NATO and European security arrangements. It also reported that key European countries were taking part in Abu Dhabi for the first time[5]. On 5 November, after meetings in Brussels with Ukrainian, NATO and European officials, Kushner announced a framework with five parts: permanent security consultations, crisis communication channels, greater military transparency, reciprocal measures to reduce the risk of escalation, and long-term arrangements for European security[6,7]. RFE/RL reported that the proposal included respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity, with "no state having a veto over another's security choices or alliances"[6]. Trump told reporters: "It's going to be a big agreement, and it's going to involve more than Ukraine"[6].

The Washington Post columnist David Ignatius reported, as relayed by Ukrainska Pravda, that "Kushner's model is the NATO-Russia Council". He also reported that Kushner wants "a simultaneous process, with both trains arriving at the same time"[12]. The NATO-Russia Council was created by the Rome Declaration of 28 May 2002, which stated that "NATO member states and Russia will work as equal partners in areas of common interest"[27]. NATO suspended practical civilian and military cooperation with Russia in April 2014 because of Russia's intervention in Ukraine and its annexation of Crimea. NATO now describes Russia as "the most significant and direct threat to Allies' security"[28].

AFP cited Russian sources as saying that settling the war was "merely one item on the agenda" and that Moscow's main objective was a review of the continent's whole security architecture[9]. TASS reported that Putin had offered to negotiate on the basis of "agreements reached in Istanbul", the understandings discussed in Anchorage and "realities on the ground"[15]. ABC News reported that earlier US attempts to win Putin over with normal relations, sanctions relief and business opportunities had failed[5].

Kyiv and its European partners object to the order of events. Zelensky told CNN that "The idea of discussing a European-Russian security infrastructure is not simple, and it can be dangerous". He argued that the war must end and Ukraine must receive guarantees first, and warned against a "Yalta 2.0". The phrase refers to the 1945 conference at which the victorious powers divided Europe into spheres of influence[7,9]. A senior official from a close US ally said: "A Ukraine peace agreement has to be settled before an agreement on security architecture"[7]. Denmark's prime minister, Mette Frederiksen, warned that "Allowing Russia to decide the borders of Europe will be a huge mistake"[9]. Samuel Charap of the RAND Corporation told ABC News: "We're not going to have a new European security architecture agreement simultaneous to a ceasefire"[5].

4Ceasefire first or settlement first

The sharpest disagreement is about what comes first. In remarks reported on 3 November, Lavrov rejected Ukrainian proposals for an immediate ceasefire and for partial truces covering energy, food, airspace and maritime activity. He said: "None of this is for peace". He added: "We insist not on a temporary ceasefire...but on reliably eliminating the root causes"[8]. In comments reported as the talks opened, he dismissed European calls for a ceasefire as an attempt to "buy time" for Kyiv, adding: "We will not agree to this"[1,2]. Reports of Russia's territorial demand differ. CBS News said Moscow wants all four regions it declared annexed in 2022, while AFP reported a demand that Ukraine give up the parts of Donetsk it still controls[1,9]. PBS NewsHour reported three further Russian positions. Russia wants Ukraine to abandon its hopes of joining NATO and to accept limits on its military. It regards any European troops sent to Ukraine after a settlement as "legitimate targets". And it insists on security guarantees for Russia itself[29].

Ukraine refuses to give up territory[2]. Zelensky said: "No one believes that Putin will stop if we give up the Donbas. He might pause — but then he will return in a year, two years or five years"[1]. On guarantees, he has drawn on the logic of NATO's collective-defence clause, asking: "If Russia starts again, what will happen? Who will do what, with what means, and when?"[29]. AFP reported that Ukrainian officials want at least ten years of financial support to maintain an army of 800,000[9]. Umerov said Ukraine was working to "ensure that Europe is a participant in the diplomatic process", which Russia opposes[26]. TASS reported that Moscow insists the talks remain strictly trilateral[15].

The European Council set out the European Union's position on 16 October 2026. Its conclusions call on Russia to "agree to a full, unconditional and immediate ceasefire". They state that "Borders must not be changed by force". They also say that the EU and its member states "stand ready to contribute to robust and credible security guarantees for Ukraine, notably through the Coalition of the Willing"[30]. The conclusions add that "Europe has a key role to play in a future settlement"[30].

Washington's public position combines optimism with caution. PBS NewsHour reported that Trump has said Moscow and Kyiv are "very close" to a deal[29]. A US official quoted by The National said: "I do believe that now we're in a position where there is a shot at trying to make serious progress to end the war"[26]. Rubio's call for Europe to take part does not fit easily with Moscow's insistence on a three-party format, or with Russia's view of European troops as potential targets.

5Strikes, casualties and contested battlefield figures

The UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission in Ukraine (HRMMU) verified at least 328 civilians killed and 2,105 injured in September 2026. That was 85% more than in September 2025, and the casualties occurred in 22 regions and the city of Kyiv[10,11]. Türk told the Human Rights Council that 15,280 civilians were killed or injured in the first eight months of 2026, 55% more than in the same period of 2025[18]. The mission counts only cases it has verified. It says the actual number in Russian-occupied areas "is likely significantly higher" because its access is limited[10]. It has also noted rising civilian casualties reported inside Russia as Ukraine stepped up its attacks there[31]. On 2 November glide bombs killed at least 31 civilians who had gathered for aid at a school in Komyshuvakha, in Zaporizhzhia region[32].

According to the HRMMU, on 30 August the Russian defence ministry announced preparations for "massive strikes against Ukrainian energy facilities", and Russian forces later resumed systematic attacks on energy infrastructure. Strikes on 13–14 October left 600,000 people in Kyiv without electricity[10,11]. On the night of 10–11 November a Russian strike cut power in parts of Kryvyi Rih and injured one person in Kharkiv and six in Zaporizhzhia[14]. Russia's defence ministry said it had hit a cargo vessel south-west of Odesa carrying weapons. It also said it had hit three electricity substations in the Kryvyi Rih and Odesa regions[3,14].

Ukraine continued its long-range drone attacks on Russian oil facilities. Alexander Drozdenko, governor of the Leningrad region, said 113 drones were shot down on the night of 9 November. The Kirishi refinery, one of Russia's largest, caught fire, and debris killed a woman in Vsevolozhsk[33]. Overnight into 11 November drones set the Ust-Luga port area on fire and injured three people. Russian officials said debris killed two people in the Smolensk region[3,14]. Figures for that night differ. France 24 reported Drozdenko as saying 92 drones were downed, while Al Jazeera, citing TASS, quoted him giving a total of 146 during an attack that was still going on[3,14].

The air war is reaching NATO territory. Overnight, Lithuania, Latvia and Estonia told residents to take shelter because of unidentified drones from Russia or Belarus. The alerts covered Kaunas, a city of about 300,000, and NATO air-defence jets were activated[3,14]. Finland briefly restricted airspace and sea access in the eastern Gulf of Finland as a "precautionary measure"[14]. Earlier in 2026 stray Ukrainian drones struck targets in Estonia and Latvia. Ukrainian officials blamed Russian electronic interference, and Latvia's defence minister and prime minister both resigned[34].

Control of territory is also disputed. On 16 October Putin claimed that Ukraine held only about 10% of the Donetsk region. However, data from the DeepState mapping project, reported by the independent Russian outlet iStories, show Russia occupying about 80%, or around 21,300 square kilometres[35].

6Competing readings of Moscow's intentions

Those who see an opening point to pressure on both sides. Foreign Policy reported that the long war has "strained Russia's wartime economy" and that Ukraine's arsenal remains "dangerously low on U.S.-made interceptors"[2]. The EU's 20th sanctions package was adopted on 23 April 2026. According to the EU's diplomatic service, it added 46 vessels to the list of banned ships, bringing the total to 632, and banned transactions with 20 Russian banks[36]. TASS noted that Kostyukov led Russia's team in January and February. This time he serves under Medinsky, alongside two deputy foreign ministers[15]. A broader delegation would fit Moscow's stated wish to discuss European security and not only the front line.

Sceptics see continuity. ABC News reported that Putin's core demands have not changed: Ukraine must surrender eastern Donbas, accept military limits and restrict European support. It also reported that Russia has intensified its bombing of Ukrainian power and water supplies[5]. RFE/RL reported that John Herbst of the Atlantic Council doubted that Russia was committed to real compromise. Glen Howard of the Saratoga Foundation warned that the proposal could set a precedent for restructuring NATO[6]. Before the talks Lavrov said: "Our position has not changed. It has never changed since the beginning of the special military operation"[15].

The consequences reach beyond Ukraine. European officials have said they would agree to a security framework with Russia only after a ceasefire, while Kushner favours running both processes at once[7,12]. A parallel process would therefore test European unity. For Kyiv, the risk Zelensky describes is that giving up territory would buy a pause, not peace[1]. For Moscow, linking a Ukrainian settlement to NATO's position in Europe would revive questions it raised in its December 2021 drafts[13]. The host also has a stake. The UAE's foreign minister, Sheikh Abdullah bin Zayed, said he hoped the talks would produce "practical steps" towards ending the war[26].

Uncertainties and open questions

Much of what happened in the negotiating room is not public. Meduza reported that Russia asked for the discussions to stay confidential, and Peskov said Moscow would not discuss security models in public[4]. Kushner's statement that the gaps had narrowed came without specifics[3,9]. Reports of Russia's territorial demand do not agree on whether it covers all four regions it claims or only the rest of Donetsk[1,9]. The most detailed account of Kushner's thinking about a model based on the NATO-Russia Council comes from a single columnist, repeated by other outlets[7,12].

Drone tallies come from Russian regional officials, and reports for 11 November give differing figures for the Leningrad region. The Russian defence ministry's account of the cargo vessel comes from a party to the war[3,14]. The UN's civilian casualty figures are verified minimums, and the monitoring mission says casualties in occupied areas are probably undercounted[10,17]. Estimates of territorial control come from open-source mapping and contradict Putin's figures[35]. A possible large prisoner exchange rests on unnamed sources cited by Meduza[4]. The result of the second day, and whether a further round will follow, were not known when the talks resumed.

Conclusion

The Abu Dhabi round reopened a channel that had been closed since February, and both Kyiv and Washington described its first day in positive terms[2,4,24]. Yet the public record shows no movement on the two questions that blocked earlier rounds. The first is whether fighting stops before a settlement. The second is who controls Donetsk and the other regions Russia claims[1,2].

What has changed is the scope of the talks. By proposing a framework for European security, Washington has raised the prospect that NATO's posture, which Russia sought to restrict in its December 2021 drafts, could be negotiated. At the same time it has promised that no state will have a veto over another's alliances[6,13]. It is not yet clear whether the wider agenda creates room for compromise or simply delays hard choices in Moscow and Kyiv. In the meantime, UN monitors continue to record civilian casualties at levels the HRMMU describes as record highs[10].
