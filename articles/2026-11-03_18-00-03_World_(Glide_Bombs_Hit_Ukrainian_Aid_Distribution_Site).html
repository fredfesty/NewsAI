Two waves of bombs on an aid queue

On the morning of Monday 2 November 2026, Russian aerial bombs struck a school in the village of Komyshuvakha, in Ukraine's southern Zaporizhzhia region, where civilians had gathered to receive humanitarian aid[1]. Local authorities had announced the distribution on social media the previous day, saying it would run from 10am to noon, and nine aerial bombs then struck the settlement in two waves[1]. President Volodymyr Zelensky said: "The Russians struck first just as people were arriving — a few aerial bombs. Then there was a second strike — more air bombs, this time on the shelter."[2,3]

The UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission in Ukraine (HRMMU) said on 3 November that the attack had killed at least 31 civilians and injured five more[1]. Mr Zelensky put the toll at 33 and said more than 50 people might still be trapped under the rubble[4]. Mykhailo Semikin, the governor of the Zaporizhzhia region, gave the number of missing as 48, while the Kyiv Post reported 58[5,6]. The dead include two State Emergency Service rescuers and four police officers[5,7]. Search teams have repeatedly had to stop work because Russian drones were approaching[6,8].

A frontline village and its reinforced school basement

Komyshuvakha lies about 20 kilometres from the front line and has been classed as a frontline settlement since November 2025, during which time Russian guided aerial bombs have hit it repeatedly[5]. The school no longer held classes because it is so close to the front, and aid was handed out there every Monday[3,7]. CNN reported Mr Semikin as saying that the structure was a newly built, EU-funded reinforced basement designed to withstand most strikes, but that the force of the bombing collapsed the concrete and started fires[9].

On 15 June 2026 a short-range drone attack on a distribution in the village injured four civilians, after which the organisation running it suspended operations in the settlement[1]. Mr Zelensky argued that "Russian forces could not have been unaware that local residents received humanitarian aid there every week"[5].

Among the deadliest attacks on civilians since 2022

The Kyiv Post reported Mr Semikin as calling it the deadliest Russian strike on the Zaporizhzhia region since the full-scale invasion of February 2022[6]. The closest regional comparison is the attack of 30 September 2022 on a civilian convoy leaving the city of Zaporizhzhia, in which, according to the then head of the regional administration, Oleksandr Starukh, S-300 missiles killed 31 people, 30 civilians and a police officer[10]. On the figures confirmed so far, the two attacks are of similar scale. Whether Komyshuvakha becomes clearly the deadlier depends on what has happened to those still missing.

The strike came less than four weeks after another mass-casualty bombing. On 8 October 2026 a strike on two passenger buses in Kramatorsk, a frontline city in the Donetsk region, killed 33 people and injured 18; according to Meduza it was probably carried out with a guided bomb[1,11].

The HRMMU, which publishes only casualties it has verified, recorded at least 437 civilians killed in July 2026, the highest monthly figure since March 2022 and 70% more than in July 2025[12]. Glide bombs became the second leading cause of civilian casualties[12]. Before Komyshuvakha, glide bombs had killed at least 478 civilians and injured 2,667 in Ukraine in 2026, or 16% of all verified civilian casualties[1]. The mission says the real numbers are likely to be higher[12].

What a three-tonne glide bomb does

Ukrainian officials have described the munitions differently. Mr Semikin said Russia used KAB-500 guided bombs and then heavier FAB-3000 bombs, which weigh about three tonnes and carry about 1.5 tonnes of explosive[9,13]. On the day of the attack Mr Zelensky referred to 10 guided bombs[14], while HNGN reported at least nine FAB-3000 bombs[8].

John Hardie of the Foundation for Defense of Democracies described the FAB-3000 M-54 as a three-tonne bomb carrying 1,400 kilograms of explosive, converted into a guided weapon by the UMPK, a gliding and correction kit[15]. Russian sources cited in his analysis gave the kit-equipped bomb a maximum range of 50 to 60 kilometres, and the UMPK allows such bombs to be launched from outside the range of most Ukrainian air defence systems[15].

The Defence Ukraine website, analysing monthly counts from Ukraine's Ministry of Defence, reported a record of 8,766 guided bombs in August 2026, an average of 283 a day[16]. The same analysis warned that a launch total cannot tell whether more targets are being struck or the same targets less efficiently[16].

Contested claims, the legal test and open questions

In a statement on 3 November, Russia's Defence Ministry said the attack had targeted Ukrainian military units in areas around Komyshuvakha and two other settlements, and that up to 25 Ukrainian servicemen had been killed[2,3]. That claim has not been independently verified[13,17]. The dead identified by name so far are rescuers, police officers and local civilians[5,13,18].

The HRMMU head, Danielle Bell, said the timing and location of the distribution had been well publicised[1,17]. "Either the Russian armed forces knew that there was a humanitarian distribution taking place, or they failed to take the most basic steps to find out what was happening in this village they were planning to attack," she said[1,17]. The rule she referred to is known as precautions in attack. It is set out in Article 57 of Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions and recognised as customary law in Rule 15 of the International Committee of the Red Cross study of customary international humanitarian law[19]. It requires parties to verify that targets are military objectives, and to cancel or suspend an attack if it becomes clear that the target is not military or that the expected harm to civilians would be excessive[19]. Any finding of a breach would depend on what Russian commanders knew and intended, and the public evidence does not yet show this.

The bombing came straight after the end of a rare local truce limited to Oleshky, a town across the Dnipro river, which the White House announced on 23 October[20,21]. Talks are still going ahead: the US special envoy, Steve Witkoff, said the United States would mediate working-level negotiations between Ukraine and Russia in Abu Dhabi on 10 and 11 November[22]. Latvia has called an extraordinary meeting of the UN Security Council[6,23].

The final death toll is not yet known, and figures for the missing range from about 20, reported by the Associated Press on the day of the attack, to 48 and 58[5,6,14]. The figure of at least 31 civilians killed, which the HRMMU cited from local authorities, is a minimum, and the mission says its verification is continuing[1]. The Russian claim has not been independently verified, and the question of whether Russian forces knew a distribution was under way or failed to check has not been answered[1,13,17]. Sources also disagree on when the Oleshky truce began and ended[14,20,24].

4What a three-tonne glide bomb does

Ukrainian officials have described the munitions differently. Mr Semikin said Russia used KAB-500 guided bombs and then heavier FAB-3000 bombs, which weigh about three tonnes and carry about 1.5 tonnes of explosive[6,18]. On the day of the attack Mr Zelensky referred to 10 guided bombs[11], while HNGN reported at least nine FAB-3000 bombs[13]. The Ukrainian outlet UA News reported that Russian media had released video of the strike, but it did not say which Russian outlet first published the footage[24]. The Kyiv Independent describes guided aerial bombs as bombs fitted with wing kits that allow them to be released from a distance[2].

John Hardie of the Foundation for Defense of Democracies set out the weapon's characteristics in a July 2024 analysis for the Long War Journal[25]. He described the FAB-3000 M-54 as a three-tonne bomb carrying 1,400 kilograms of explosive, converted into a guided weapon by the UMPK, a gliding and correction kit made by Russia's Tactical Missiles Corporation[25]. Russian sources cited in the analysis gave the kit-equipped bomb a maximum range of 50 to 60 kilometres, and the UMPK allows such bombs to be launched from outside the range of most Ukrainian air defence systems[25]. A Russian pilot claimed a circular error probable of 10 metres; this is the radius within which half of the bombs land. From the footage available, Mr Hardie judged that the true figure was probably about 30 metres[25]. Russia began using the weapon in combat in June 2024[25]. The 1,400-kilogram charge in that analysis is slightly below the 1.5 tonnes cited by Ukrainian officials.

The Defence Ukraine website, analysing monthly counts from Ukraine's Ministry of Defence, reported a record of 8,766 guided bombs in August 2026, an average of 283 a day[26]. The same analysis warned that a launch total cannot tell whether more targets are being struck or the same targets less efficiently. It cited an estimate by the independent researcher Clément Molin that about one guided bomb in two now falls in open ground[26]. On supply, the Kyiv-based Center for Defense Reforms reported that in 2025 a Russian procurement firm bought nearly 278,000 servo drives, enough for about 139,000 munitions[27]. It also found that only eight of the 20 companies it identified in the production chain faced Western sanctions[27]. All of these figures come from Ukrainian official or research sources and have not been independently audited.

5Moscow's military claim and the duty of precaution

In a statement on 3 November, Russia's Defence Ministry said the attack had targeted Ukrainian military units in areas around Komyshuvakha and two other settlements in the Zaporizhzhia region[5,8]. It said up to 25 Ukrainian servicemen had been killed and that 15 vehicles and an electronic warfare station had been destroyed[5,8]. That claim has not been independently verified[6,7]. The dead identified by name so far are rescuers, police officers and local civilians[2,6,12]. On the day of the attack RFE/RL reported no immediate official Russian comment[17]. Both Russia and Ukraine deny deliberately targeting civilians[8].

The HRMMU head, Danielle Bell, said the timing and location of the distribution had been well publicised[1,7]. "Either the Russian armed forces knew that there was a humanitarian distribution taking place, or they failed to take the most basic steps to find out what was happening in this village they were planning to attack," she said[1,7]. She added: "In either case, this attack seems to have violated the most fundamental principles of international humanitarian law."[7]

The rule Ms Bell was referring to is known as precautions in attack. It is set out in Article 57 of Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions and recognised as customary law in Rule 15 of the International Committee of the Red Cross study of customary international humanitarian law[28]. It requires parties to verify that targets are military objectives, and to cancel or suspend an attack if it becomes clear that the target is not military or that the expected harm to civilians would be excessive[28]. Any finding of a breach would depend on what Russian commanders knew and intended, and the public evidence does not yet show this. In a separate report dated 26 October 2026, the UN Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine, chaired by Erik Møse, concluded that Russian attacks on Ukraine's energy infrastructure amounted to war crimes and crimes against humanity[29].

Kyiv's response has been blunt. Mr Zelensky called the attack "a vile terrorist strike", and Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha called it "another barbaric and cynical Russian attack"[8]. Mr Zelensky also pointed to the timing, saying: "It is very telling that all of this is happening on the day of the US congressional elections."[6]

6A lapsed truce, new talks and the Security Council

The bombing came straight after the end of a rare local truce. On 23 October the White House announced that Melania Trump, the US first lady, had secured a ceasefire limited to Oleshky, a town across the Dnipro river, to let aid in and civilians out[30,31]. It said an estimated 1,700 to 2,000 residents remained there, out of a pre-war population of 24,000[30]. AFP described Oleshky as Russian-occupied and reported that the truce was overseen by Russia's human rights commissioner, Yana Lantratova, and her Ukrainian counterpart, Dmytro Lubinets[32]. The White House gave 29 October as the start date, but RFE/RL and the Associated Press reported that the pause began on 27 October[11,17,30]. Accounts of when it ended also differ. RFE/RL said it ended late on 1 November when Russia refused to extend it, and the Associated Press said it expired on 2 November[11,17]. Reuters described it as a six-day truce, and Euronews as the longest of the war[5,31].

Ukraine's military said Russia had used the pause to strengthen its positions and resupply[11,17]. Mr Peskov, suggesting that both sides had done the same, said: "This is a completely normal practice in such situations."[17,32] Mr Zelensky said: "There was a moment when the next steps could have shown that progress toward peace was possible," adding that the attack sent "an entirely opposite signal."[31] On the night of 2 to 3 November, according to Ukrainian figures, Russia launched 289 attack drones, 17 ballistic missiles and 59 cruise missiles, mainly at energy facilities[6,13]. Euronews reported 25 people wounded at a medical facility in Kharkiv, where about 200 patients were evacuated[31].

Talks are still going ahead. The US special envoy, Steve Witkoff, said the United States would mediate working-level negotiations between Ukraine and Russia in Abu Dhabi on 10 and 11 November, covering post-war security arrangements, de-escalation, humanitarian efforts and next steps towards peace[10]. Earlier rounds in Abu Dhabi in January and February 2026 and in Geneva in February did not produce a comprehensive peace agreement[10]. Mr Peskov confirmed that Russia would take part[13]. Latvia, which in November holds the rotating presidency of the UN Security Council for the first time, has called an extraordinary meeting of the council[4,9]. Its foreign minister, Baiba Braže, cited "horrific Russian attacks on civilians in Ukraine, including on an aid distribution centre in Komyshuvakha"[9].

Uncertainties and open questions

The final death toll is not yet known. Figures for the missing range from about 20, reported by the Associated Press on the day of the attack[11], to 48 given by Mr Semikin[2] and 58 reported by the Kyiv Post[4]. Some of the people listed as missing may already be among those later confirmed dead. The figure of at least 31 civilians killed, which the HRMMU cited from local authorities, is a minimum, and the mission says its verification is continuing[1].

The number and type of bombs rest on Ukrainian official statements, which give nine or 10 bombs and name both KAB-500 and FAB-3000 munitions[6,11,13]. The Russian Defence Ministry's claim to have hit military units and killed up to 25 servicemen has not been independently verified[6,7]. The central legal question, whether Russian forces knew a distribution was under way or failed to check, is the one Ms Bell raised and has not been answered[1]. Sources disagree on when the Oleshky truce began and ended[11,17,30]. Monthly glide-bomb counts come from Ukraine's Ministry of Defence, a party to the war, and analysts warn that launch totals are an imperfect measure of effect[26].

Conclusion

The core facts of the Komyshuvakha strike come from an independent UN monitor. At least 31 civilians were killed on 2 November when bombs struck a school during a publicly announced aid distribution[1]. Ukrainian officials put the toll higher and say dozens of people remain missing[2,3]. Russia says it struck Ukrainian military units, a claim that has not been independently verified[5,6]. Any legal finding is likely to turn on what the attackers knew, or should have found out, about activity in the village, which is the test Ms Bell set out[1]. The attack follows a year in which glide bombs had already killed at least 478 civilians in Ukraine, and in which UN monitors reported glide bombs becoming the second leading cause of civilian casualties[1,23]. It also comes about a week before US-mediated talks in Abu Dhabi, and Latvia has called an extraordinary meeting of the UN Security Council[9,10]. The next things that will shape the record are whether those meetings deal with attacks on aid distributions and how many of the missing are found alive.
