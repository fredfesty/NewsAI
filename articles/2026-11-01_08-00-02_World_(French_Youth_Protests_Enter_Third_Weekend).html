A third weekend of marches and clashes

Hundreds of thousands of people marched across France on Saturday 31 October, the third Saturday in a row of mass protest against the cost of living, and police fired tear gas and used water cannon against groups that confronted them[1,2,3]. The interior ministry put national turnout at about 320,000, including 70,000 in Paris, while youth unions claimed nearly 500,000 nationwide and 200,000 in the capital[1,2]. On the ministry's count, turnout was lower than on 24 October, when it recorded 410,000, and on 17 October, when it recorded 550,000[1]. About 70,000 police and gendarmes were deployed to prevent a repeat of the looting of 17 October[2,4].

The violence was scattered. In Lyon, people threw projectiles at police, who answered with tear gas, and in Toulouse officers used water cannon on an unauthorised protest of several thousand mostly young people[3]. In Paris, France 24 reported that masked demonstrators prised up paving stones and threw them at officers[2]. Police made more than 290 arrests during the day, and the Associated Press, cited by CBS News, reported more than 560 by Saturday night[2,3]. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez said the demonstrations had been largely orderly but called the violence of the rioting groups extraordinary, and he blamed far-left extremists[2]. An administrative court upheld a ban on the route organisers had planned through central Paris[2].

From school blockades to a national movement

The movement began over schools, not prices. Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International both date the start of the high school protests to 21 September 2026[5,6]. Human Rights Watch says the pupils were protesting against chronic underfunding, teacher shortages, overcrowded classrooms and deteriorating school buildings[6]. By 29 September, Euronews reported, blockades that began in the Île-de-France region had spread to Lille, Nantes, Marseille, Bordeaux, Lyon and Rennes[7]. According to interior ministry figures reported by CNBC, 1,027 of France's roughly 3,700 lycées were affected on 1 October, and 222 of them were blockaded[8]. On 6 October, between 250,000 and 450,000 people demonstrated, according to the official and organisers' counts respectively[9].

Injuries to minors made policing part of the dispute. In Lens, a 15-year-old lost a hand after police fired a stun grenade, and on 7 October Mr Nuñez suspended the use of stun grenades at student protests[10]. By then, 6,100 people had been arrested since the protests began, overwhelmingly minors, and 715 police officers and 215 teenagers had been injured[10]. The movement then took in a wider set of grievances. Trade unions and the gilets jaunes, or yellow vests, joined the students, and the demands extended to the cost of living, fuel prices and the state of public services[1,4]. After the security crackdown and serious injuries to some protesters, the movement added protection of civil liberties and of the right to protest[3].

The riots of 17 October and a shifting official toll

On Saturday 17 October, marches against rising living costs and education funding began peacefully, then turned into some of the worst unrest France had seen in years[11]. Masked youths set fires, looted shops and attacked public buildings in cities including Paris, Marseille, Nice, Bordeaux, Lille and Strasbourg[11]. Turnout estimates ranged from the authorities' 550,000 to the organisers' 800,000[11].

The official toll changed sharply over the following days. Reports compiled on 18 October gave 1,200 arrests and 150 injured police officers[11]. Al Jazeera later reported nearly 2,400 arrests and about 1,000 injured police officers and gendarmes[12]. A week afterwards, citing interior ministry figures, Anadolu Agency gave 2,628 arrests and 1,144 injured officers, and reported 1,522 businesses looted[13].

The government blamed organised radicals. Mr Nuñez attributed the violence to "several thousand ultra-left individuals"[14]. President Emmanuel Macron expressed "anger and shame" and promised that rioters and looters would pay for their crimes[12]. On 26 October, according to an agency report carried by La Dernière Heure, he admitted that the authorities had not anticipated the 17 October mobilisation and that police numbers had not been properly sized for it[15]. For 24 October the government raised the national deployment to about 70,000 officers, 20 per cent more than the week before[16]. Anadolu, citing the interior ministry, reported 410,000 marchers, 1,314 arrests and 24 looted businesses, and Mr Nuñez presented the fall in looting as evidence that the security measures had worked[13].

Energy prices, stagnation and a narrow budget

Official statistics show the economic grievances behind the marches. INSEE estimates provisionally that consumer prices in October 2026 were 3.4% higher than a year earlier, up from 3.1% in September, with energy prices 24.1% higher[17]. In September, INSEE recorded annual rises of 36.2% for petroleum products and 44.1% for diesel[18]. The Local, reporting an AFP analysis, said average diesel prices hit a record €2.41 per litre on 20 September and linked the rise to the war that began in February 2026, when the United States and Israel launched military operations against Iran[19]. INSEE estimated that household purchasing power per consumption unit fell by 0.6% in the second quarter of 2026, and its first estimate for the third quarter shows output falling by 0.1%[20,21].

The state has little money to offer. INSEE puts public debt at 119.0% of GDP at the end of June 2026[22]. On 17 September Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu announced €54 billion of savings for 2027 and a deficit target of 5% of GDP[23]. On 30 October, Fitch Ratings affirmed France's A+ rating but revised its outlook to negative[24,25]. Investing.com reports that Fitch expects debt to reach roughly 124% of GDP by 2028, and Fitch said social friction made deficit reduction harder[24].

Contested policing, rival explanations and unresolved questions

Human rights organisations argue that the policing has been disproportionate. On 14 October Human Rights Watch said more than 6,800 people had been detained, about 86% of them children, and documented the case of a 14-year-old whose jaw was shattered by a tear-gas canister fired at close range[6]. On 28 October four UN special rapporteurs called the injuries to minors "unacceptable and unjustifiable" and judged detentions affecting "several thousand people, mainly minors" disproportionate[26]. Ministers reject this account. They say organised radical groups, not students, were behind the violence, and they have defended police conduct[14,27].

The government's main legislative answer is the "casseurs-payeurs" bill. As toughened by the Senate, it makes anyone who knowingly took part in a gathering where violence or damage occurred jointly liable for that damage, up to €10,000 per person[28,29]. The Senate adopted it by 229 votes to 105, and it now goes to the National Assembly[28]. Amnesty International France urged the government to drop the bill[30].

Others offer different readings. The hard-left candidate Jean-Luc Mélenchon said the government was "solely responsible" for the destruction of 17 October because it had failed to organise public order[12]. After meeting Mr Lecornu on 21 October, Sophie Binet of the CGT union said he had announced no measures and that "the time has come to move the mobilisation up a gear"[31]. The protests have also spread beyond France, with NBC News reporting protests in more than 30 German cities on 31 October[4]. Alberto Alemanno, a professor of EU law at HEC Paris, said the European protests have varied causes and that calling them a "teenage revolution" is "deeply misleading"[32].

Much remains uncertain. Crowd sizes depend on who counts them, and none of the sources consulted explains how either side's count was made[2,13]. Official arrest figures for 17 October rose from about 1,200 to 2,628 in successive reports[11,12,13,14]. The liability bill has not yet been examined by the National Assembly, so its final form is unknown[28]. Neither the official figures nor the rights groups' reports show whether the movement will fade or harden in the weeks ahead.

1Halloween marches end in tear gas and arrests

Hundreds of thousands of people marched across France on Saturday 31 October. It was the third Saturday in a row of mass protest against the cost of living, and police fired tear gas and used water cannon against groups that confronted them as the marches ended[1,4,5]. The interior ministry put national turnout at about 320,000, including 70,000 in Paris. Youth unions claimed nearly 500,000 nationwide and 200,000 in the capital[1,4]. On the ministry's own count, turnout was lower than on 24 October, when it recorded 410,000, and lower than on 17 October, when it recorded 550,000[1]. About 70,000 police and gendarmes were deployed to prevent a repeat of the looting of 17 October[4,16].

The violence was scattered but widespread. In Lyon, people threw projectiles at police, who answered with tear gas. In Toulouse, officers used water cannon on an unauthorised protest of several thousand mostly young people, some of them in Halloween costume, and in Marseille demonstrators clashed with police[5]. In Paris, France 24 reported that masked demonstrators prised up paving stones and threw them at officers, and it described a hard core of several hundred rioters[4]. Police made more than 290 arrests during the day. The Associated Press, cited by CBS News, reported more than 560 by Saturday night[4,5]. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez said the demonstrations had been largely orderly but called the violence of the rioting groups extraordinary. He blamed far-left extremists and described a Molotov cocktail attack on a riot police officer in Paris, who suffered minor injuries, as attempted murder[4].

Restrictions announced in advance shaped the day. An administrative court upheld a ban on the route organisers had planned through central Paris, which kept the march on the eastern side of the city. During the week, student groups had complained that police refused to authorise a march through the centre[4,5]. Mr Nuñez warned that wearing a Halloween mask with malicious intent carried a €150 fine. The authorities also said they had found posts on social media urging protesters to spark a "revolution" during Halloween gatherings[4,5]. "Security forces held their ground and protected city centres," he said afterwards[1].

The protests matter beyond public order. According to CBS News, drawing on AFP, they come less than six months before a presidential election in which the far-right leader Marine Le Pen leads the polls. They also come with the euro area's second-largest economy close to recession and under pressure to cut its debt, which leaves the government little room to ease tensions through extra spending[5]. Al Jazeera presented the weekend as the run-up to an expected general strike[17]. Student unions called for new blockades of high schools and universities the following week, and trade unions have called a day of strikes and protests for 5 November[4,18].

The movement began over schools, not prices. Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International both date the start of the high school protests to 21 September 2026[2,3]. Human Rights Watch says the pupils were protesting against chronic underfunding, teacher shortages, overcrowded classrooms and deteriorating school buildings[3]. By 29 September, Euronews reported, blockades that began in the Île-de-France region around Paris had spread to Lille, Nantes, Marseille, Bordeaux, Lyon and Rennes. Pupils complained of buildings that overheated during heatwaves, crowded classes and timetables that gave them, in one student's words, "more hours of lessons than sleep"[19]. Mr Nuñez reported 164 arrests and 180 affected schools on 28 September and said: "We cannot tolerate excesses."[19]. The blockades coincided with strikes by teachers and firefighters against the draft budget of Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu[19].

The movement grew quickly. According to interior ministry figures reported by CNBC, 1,027 of France's roughly 3,700 lycées, or high schools, were affected on 1 October, and 222 of them were blockaded. More than 400 schools closed the next day[20]. Education Minister Édouard Geffray called the movement "legitimate at the outset", while the interior ministry said the unrest had the characteristics of "urban violence"[20]. On 6 October, between 250,000 and 450,000 people demonstrated, according to the official and organisers' counts respectively[21].

Injuries to minors made policing itself part of the dispute. In Lens, a 15-year-old lost a hand after police fired a stun grenade. On 7 October Mr Nuñez suspended the use of stun grenades at student protests[22]. By then, according to figures reported by AFP and Reuters, 6,100 people had been arrested since the protests began, overwhelmingly minors. More than 1,800 had been prosecuted, 1,483 of them minors, 715 police officers and 215 teenagers had been injured, and 18 internal investigations into alleged police misconduct had been opened[22]. On 6 October Mr Lecornu defended a firm approach. He rejected accusations of police violence, called the incidents accidents, and accused the hard-left party La France Insoumise (LFI) of stoking the revolt[23]. Mathilde Panot, who leads LFI in parliament, accused the government of unprecedented repression. She called for a ban on what she called mutilating weapons and cited a young person who lost an eye in Tours[23].

The movement then took in a wider set of grievances. Trade unions and the gilets jaunes, or yellow vests, joined the students, and the demands extended to the cost of living, fuel prices and the state of public services[1,16]. CBS News reported that, after the security crackdown and serious injuries to some protesters, the movement added protection of civil liberties and of the right to protest to its demands[5].

3The riots of 17 October and a rising official toll

On Saturday 17 October, student organisations marched with trade unions and the yellow vests. Marches against rising living costs and education funding began peacefully, then turned into some of the worst unrest France had seen in years[6]. Turnout estimates ranged from the authorities' 550,000 to the organisers' 800,000[6]. Masked youths set fires, looted shops and attacked public buildings in cities including Paris, Marseille, Nice, Bordeaux, Lille and Strasbourg. Emmanuel Grégoire, a deputy mayor of Paris, said "whole streets have been devastated"[6]. Mr Lecornu said it was "a miracle the toll isn't higher"[6].

The official toll changed sharply over the following days, which matters for anyone who uses these figures. On 18 October, reports compiled from AFP, AP and Reuters gave 1,200 arrests and 150 injured police officers[6]. Mr Nuñez then said more than 1,800 people had been arrested and that about 500 police officers, firefighters and paramedics had been injured[7]. When President Emmanuel Macron addressed the country, Al Jazeera reported nearly 2,400 arrests and about 1,000 injured police officers and gendarmes[8]. A week later, Turkey's state-run Anadolu Agency, citing interior ministry figures, gave 2,628 arrests and 1,144 injured officers for 17 October. It also reported 1,522 businesses looted and 2,025 fire-related call-outs[9]. The rising numbers may reflect arrests and injuries being recorded over time, but none of the sources consulted explains the revisions.

The government blamed organised radicals. Mr Nuñez attributed the violence to "several thousand ultra-left individuals" and said France "will not yield to those who want to impose disorder and violence"[7]. Mr Macron expressed "anger and shame" and promised that rioters and looters would pay for their crimes[8]. On 26 October, according to an agency report carried by the Belgian daily La Dernière Heure, he admitted that the authorities had not anticipated the 17 October mobilisation at all and that police numbers had not been properly sized for it[24].

For 24 October, the government raised the national deployment to about 70,000 officers, 20 per cent more than the week before. In Paris it refused a permit for the unions' planned march route and offered another[25]. Anadolu, citing the interior ministry, reported 410,000 marchers, including 110,000 in Paris, against union estimates of about 800,000 and 250,000. It counted 1,314 arrests, 53 injured security personnel and 24 looted businesses[9]. Mr Nuñez presented the fall in looting as evidence that the security measures had worked[9].

4Energy prices, stagnation and a narrow budget

Official statistics show the economic grievances behind the marches. INSEE, the national statistics institute, estimates provisionally that consumer prices in October 2026 were 3.4% higher than a year earlier, up from 3.1% in September. The harmonised index used for comparisons across the euro area rose 3.8%[10]. Energy drove the rise, with prices 24.1% higher year on year, while food inflation slowed to 1.2% and services inflation stood at 2.7%[10]. In September, INSEE recorded annual rises of 36.2% for petroleum products and 44.1% for diesel, and annual inflation had been 2.4% in August[26]. INSEE says the October figures are preliminary, should not be used for contractual revaluations, and will be finalised on 13 November[10].

The energy shock began abroad. The Local, reporting an AFP analysis of economy ministry data, said average diesel prices hit a record €2.41 per litre on 20 September. It linked the rise to the war that began in February 2026, when the United States and Israel launched military operations against Iran, and to renewed conflict in Yemen and attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure. Brent crude stayed above $100 a barrel[27]. The same report noted the government's fear of a repeat of the 2018 yellow vest protests, which were triggered by fuel price rises[27].

Households have felt the effect in their incomes. INSEE estimated that household purchasing power per consumption unit fell by 0.6% in the second quarter of 2026 as consumer prices accelerated[28]. Its first estimate for the third quarter, published on 30 October, shows output falling by 0.1% after no growth in the second quarter. Investment fell by 0.6% and exports by 1.7%, although household consumption rose by 0.4%[11].

The state has little money to offer. INSEE puts the 2025 public deficit at 5.1% of GDP and public debt at 115.6%; by the end of June 2026 debt had reached 119.0% of GDP, or €3,595.5 billion[12,29]. On 17 September Mr Lecornu announced €54 billion of savings for 2027 and a deficit target of 5% of GDP. The plan keeps the civil service pay index, frozen since mid-2023, frozen again, which saves about €2 billion[30]. France is also subject to the European Union's excessive deficit procedure[20].

On 30 October, Fitch Ratings affirmed France's A+ rating but revised its outlook to negative[13,14]. Investing.com reports that Fitch expects debt to reach roughly 124% of GDP by 2028 and a 2027 deficit of 5.5%. Fitch also cut its growth forecasts to 0.5% for 2026 and 0.9% for 2027, and said social friction made deficit reduction harder[13]. Figures for bond yields differ. Investing.com gives a ten-year yield of 4.9%, while Global Banking & Finance Review cites yields above 5.14%, the highest since 2002[13,14].

5Rights groups, the United Nations and the liability bill

Human rights organisations argue that the policing has been disproportionate. On 5 October Amnesty International cited more than 5,000 arrests, 85% of them involving minors according to the authorities, and called for de-escalation[2]. Anne Savinel-Barras, president of Amnesty International France, said that "children and young people peacefully demanding their right to education must be protected rather than being beaten, tear-gassed"[2]. On 14 October Human Rights Watch said more than 6,800 people had been detained, about 86% of them children. It documented the case of a 14-year-old whose jaw was shattered by a tear-gas canister fired at close range[3]. It noted that 36 internal investigations had been opened but said such reviews rarely lead to real accountability[3].

United Nations officials have raised similar concerns. On 20 October the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, Volker Türk, condemned all acts of violence and deplored "the actions of those exploiting protests to commit acts of violence and vandalism against public and private property". He added that "allegations of unlawful use of force must be investigated promptly, effectively and independently"[31]. UN News reported that he was concerned by reports of unnecessary and excessive use of force[32]. On 28 October four UN special rapporteurs, including Gina Romero, the rapporteur on freedom of peaceful assembly, and Farida Shaheed, issued a joint statement. They called the "serious and, in some cases, irreversible" injuries to minors "unacceptable and unjustifiable". They also judged detentions affecting "several thousand people, mainly minors" disproportionate[15]. These experts serve in a personal capacity and do not speak for the UN as a whole[15].

Ministers reject this account. They say organised radical groups, not students, were behind the violence, and they have defended police conduct[7,23].

The government's main legislative answer is a bill on damage done in public spaces, known as "casseurs-payeurs", roughly "wreckers pay"[33]. As toughened by the Senate, it makes anyone who knowingly took part in a gathering where violence or damage occurred jointly liable for that damage, up to €10,000 per person. Liability does not depend on proof that the person caused the damage, and family and housing benefits can be seized to recover the sums[33,34]. The Senate adopted the bill by 229 votes to 105 on the night of 27 to 28 October, and it now goes to the National Assembly[33]. Nathalie Godard of Amnesty International France urged the government to drop the bill, saying it seriously threatens the right to protest. Amnesty argues that international law allows people to be held liable only for their own conduct[35].

6Rival explanations and a protest wave crossing borders

Opposition leaders interpret the unrest differently. Jean-Luc Mélenchon, the hard-left presidential candidate, said the government was "solely responsible" for the destruction of 17 October because it had failed to organise public order. He has accused Mr Macron's government of "taking an increasingly authoritarian turn"[5,8]. The far-right politician Jordan Bardella called Mr Macron's address "lamentable" and "cowardly"[8]. Polls cited by AFP in late October put Ms Le Pen at 33 to 35 per cent in the first round of the election[25].

Trade unions present the movement as a dispute over wages and living standards. After meeting Mr Lecornu on 21 October, Sophie Binet, general secretary of the CGT union, said he had announced no measures and that "the time has come to move the mobilisation up a gear"[18]. Cyril Chabanier of the CFTC union said the government had opened the door to talks on the minimum wage[18]. Protesters on 31 October tied prices to wider political concerns. "We say no to the cost of living, no to the far right, yes to high school students' demands," said Camille Guerin, 31, in Nantes[5]. One marcher told France 24 that families were struggling over a few cents while leaders juggled billions[1].

The protests have also spread beyond France. On 31 October, NBC News reported protests organised by the German initiative Gen Z regelt in more than 30 cities, with property damage and looting in Frankfurt. Italy deployed 500 soldiers alongside police in Milan, and police used water cannon in Rome and Brussels[16]. The German organisers modelled their protests on the French movement and added opposition to possible military conscription to demands on schools and living costs[36]. UN Secretary-General António Guterres said on 22 October that "pressures are building over the price of food, fuel and other basics" and that the "legitimate grievances of the protestors cannot be overlooked"[37].

Some specialists warn against treating these protests as a single event. Alberto Alemanno, a professor of EU law at HEC Paris, told Euronews that the European protests have varied causes and that calling them a "teenage revolution" is "deeply misleading"[38]. Misleading material online makes the protests harder to assess. Eunews reported that NewsGuard had identified viral videos presented as protest footage that in fact showed unrelated events from 2017 and 2023[21].

Uncertainties and open questions

Several central facts remain unsettled. Crowd sizes depend on who counts them. For 31 October the interior ministry gave 320,000 and youth unions nearly 500,000, and for 24 October the gap was 410,000 against about 800,000[4,9]. None of the sources consulted explains how either side's count was made. Official arrest figures for 17 October rose from about 1,200 to 2,628 in successive reports, and detention totals differ: Amnesty International gave more than 5,000 on 5 October and Human Rights Watch more than 6,800 on 14 October[2,3,6,7,8,9]. The figure of more than 560 arrests on 31 October comes from the Associated Press, and no final ministry figure appeared in the sources consulted[5]. Mr Nuñez's description of the Molotov cocktail attack as attempted murder is a minister's characterisation, not a court finding[4]. The estimate of more than €1 billion in retail losses comes from the Chamber of Commerce, and the sources do not describe how it was calculated[1]. INSEE's October inflation estimate is provisional[10]. Ministers and the movement's supporters still dispute who was behind the violence[7,8]. The liability bill has not yet been examined by the National Assembly, so its final form is unknown[33].

Conclusion

Three weeks of Saturday marches have turned a dispute over school conditions into a national confrontation over living costs, policing and the right to protest. The government can point to official figures showing that turnout and looting have fallen since 17 October[1,9]. Its critics, including Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch and UN special rapporteurs, point to injured minors and thousands of detentions[2,3,15]. On either reading, money is tight. Energy-driven inflation, a shrinking economy and public debt of 119% of GDP leave little room for concessions[5,10,11,12]. The next tests are close: the 5 November strike, the liability bill's passage through the National Assembly, and an April presidential election in which the far right leads the polls[1,18,33]. Neither the official figures nor the rights groups' reports show whether the movement will fade or harden in the weeks ahead.
