A Democratic wave carrying a series of firsts

Voters in the United States on 3 November 2026 elected a series of candidates who will be the first of their group to hold particular offices, according to a tally by the Associated Press (AP)[1]. Democrat Keisha Lance Bottoms won the governorship of Georgia and became the first Black woman elected governor of any US state[1,2,3]. In New Hampshire, Chris Pappas won an open Senate seat and will be the first openly gay man to serve in the Senate[1,2,4]. In the House of Representatives, Hakeem Jeffries is positioned to become the first Black Speaker after his party was projected to win a majority[1,5].

The milestones came within a broad Democratic victory. NBC News projected that Democrats had won control of both the House and the Senate[6,7]. Its Senate count stood at 54 Democratic seats to 44 Republican[6]. Republican Byron Donalds won the Florida governorship and will be the state's first Black governor[8]. Democrat Deb Haaland won in New Mexico and told supporters she stood before them "as the first Native American woman elected governor in U.S. history"[1].

Georgia and Florida and the short record of Black governors

Bottoms defeated Republican Rick Jackson, a billionaire healthcare executive who, according to the AP, spent more than $200 million of his own fortune on the race[3,9]. AP vote counts compiled by MultiState gave Bottoms 54.3% to Jackson's 45.7%[10]. She is the first Democrat to win the Georgia governorship since 1998[3].

The Center for American Women and Politics (CAWP) at Rutgers University noted in March 2026 that no Black woman had ever served as governor of a US state, and that Georgia had never had a woman governor[11]. According to the AP, only three Black governors had previously been elected in US history, all of them Democratic men[3]. Georgia's 83 previous governors had all been white men[12].

In Florida, Donalds beat Democrat David Jolly by 50.0% to 48.7%[13]. The AP described the contest as unexpectedly competitive in a state where Republicans hold a voter-registration advantage of about 1.5 million[8]. Because Black candidates from both parties won governorships on the same night, the firsts cannot be read as a purely partisan story.

Congress changes hands and the Speakership awaits a vote

Estimates of the size of the new House majority varied on 4 November because many races had not yet been called. NBC News showed 231 seats called for Democrats, 144 for Republicans and 60 uncalled, and estimated a final Democratic total of about 258 seats[7]. CNBC reported that Democrats were projected to win at least 250 seats[14]. A House majority requires 218 seats[7,15]. CNBC reported Democrats on track for at least 54 Senate seats, while MS NOW reported that they had flipped enough Republican-held seats to reach the 51 needed for outright control[14,15].

Voters do not choose the Speaker. CNBC reported that Jeffries would need the support of a majority of the Democratic caucus after the new Congress is seated in January[5]. According to his official biography, he was unanimously elected Democratic Leader by his colleagues in 2022 and 2024[16].

In New Hampshire, Pappas beat John E. Sununu, a former Republican senator, for the seat left vacant by the retirement of the Democrat Jeanne Shaheen[4]. NBC's results gave Pappas 56.2% to Sununu's 41.9%[6]. According to the AP, Democrat Peggy Flanagan, a citizen of the White Earth Band of Ojibwe, won in Minnesota to become the first Native American woman elected to the Senate[1].

Ohio and the Indian-American candidates

In Ohio, Democrat Amy Acton won 52.0% of the vote to Vivek Ramaswamy's 44.7%, according to figures compiled by MultiState[17]. Ramaswamy, the Indian-American Republican, had sought the governorship with Trump's endorsement[18,19]. The AP reported that Acton is the first Democrat to win the Ohio governorship in 20 years and the first woman elected to the post[19]. More than $100 million was spent against her, and Elon Musk gave at least $5 million to a political action committee aligned with Ramaswamy[19]. NBC News reported that, as an Indian American of Hindu faith running in a mainly white and Christian state, Ramaswamy faced online racism and bigotry[18].

Several Indian-American Democrats won House seats. Pia Dandiya won Florida's newly redrawn 22nd congressional district, defeating the Republican Casey Askar, and Business Today put her share of the vote at 51.85%[20,21]. Both outlets describe her as the first Indian American to represent Florida in the House[20,21]. Suhas Subramanyam won a second term in Virginia's 10th district with 63.16%[21,22]. Pramila Jayapal won a sixth term in Washington's 7th district with 87.5%[21,22].

In Pew's survey, 68% of Indian-American registered voters aligned with the Democratic Party and 29% with the Republican Party[23]. That partisan lean fits a 2026 pattern in which Indian-American Democrats won while the most prominent Indian-American Republican lost, though a single election cannot show why.

Representation and the meaning of the firsts

CAWP's final nominee data counted 26 women nominated for governor, a record 35.1% of all gubernatorial nominees[24]. Democrat Gina Hinojosa beat the Republican governor Greg Abbott by 52.9% to 45.6% in Texas and will be the state's first Latina governor[2,25]. Haaland beat Republican Gregg Hull by 57.2% to 42.8%[2,26].

Opinions differ on what the firsts mean. Euronews quoted Bill Shiebler of State Voices as saying: "We're witnessing an electorate that increasingly sees itself in its leaders"[2]. After Democratic wins in November 2025, Ramaswamy urged Republicans to stop identity-focused political appeals[27]. Pappas himself said "I'm not running to make history"[1]. In 1999 the political scientist Jane Mansbridge offered a contingent defence of descriptive representation, arguing that in some contexts "the better communication and experiential knowledge of descriptive representatives enhances their substantive representation"[28].

Several results rest on incomplete counts. On 4 November NBC News still listed 60 House races as uncalled[7]. The Alaska Senate race, in which Democrat Mary Peltola could become the first Alaska Native senator, was too early to call[1,6]. Mississippi's Senate race was also uncalled[6,14]. Outlets differed on Michigan, as the AP listed Abdul El-Sayed as the first Muslim senator pending final results while Euronews described him as still competing[1,2]. Jeffries's speakership depends on a House vote in January[5]. Whether the new office holders change what government does will take far longer to judge.

1A Democratic wave that carried a series of firsts

Voters in the United States on 3 November 2026 elected a series of candidates who will be the first of their group to hold particular offices, according to a tally by the Associated Press (AP)[1]. Democrat Keisha Lance Bottoms, a former mayor of Atlanta, won the governorship of Georgia and became the first Black woman elected governor of any US state[1,2,3]. In New Hampshire, the Democratic congressman Chris Pappas won an open Senate seat and will be the first openly gay man to serve in the Senate[1,2,4]. In the House of Representatives, the Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries is positioned to become the first Black Speaker after his party was projected to win a majority[1,5].

The milestones came within a broad Democratic victory. NBC News projected that Democrats had won control of both the House and the Senate[6,7]. Its Senate count stood at 54 Democratic seats to 44 Republican, with seats taken from Republicans in Iowa, Kansas, Maine, North Carolina, Ohio, South Carolina and Texas[6]. President Donald Trump responded by posting on his Truth Social platform an image of a red cap bearing the word "VETO", and he told The New York Times that his administration would "work with the Democrats", CNBC reported[13].

The firsts were not confined to one party. Republican Byron Donalds won the Florida governorship and will be the state's first Black governor, which the AP describes as making him the nation's first elected Black Republican governor[8]. Democrat Deb Haaland, a citizen of Laguna Pueblo, won in New Mexico and told supporters: "I stand before you as the first Native American woman elected governor"[1].

Not every prominent candidate from a minority group succeeded. Vivek Ramaswamy, the Indian-American Republican who sought the Ohio governorship with Trump's endorsement, lost to Democrat Amy Acton, who becomes the first woman elected governor of Ohio[10,11]. Alexis Anderson-Reed said: "These firsts show how our country is changing"[1]. The returns alone cannot show whether these milestones reflect a lasting change in whom American voters choose, or mainly the size of one party's swing in a single year.

2Georgia and the short record of Black governors

Bottoms defeated Republican Rick Jackson, a billionaire healthcare executive who, according to the AP, spent more than $200 million of his own fortune on the race[3,14]. AP vote counts compiled by the MultiState elections tracker gave Bottoms 54.3% (2,268,462 votes) to Jackson's 45.7% (1,908,261 votes), a margin of 8.6 percentage points, with the race called on the morning of 4 November[15]. She is the first Democrat to win the Georgia governorship since 1998[3]. Bottoms was mayor of Atlanta from 2018 to 2022 and later served as a senior adviser in the Biden administration[14].

The historical record explains why the result drew attention. The Center for American Women and Politics (CAWP) at Rutgers University noted in March 2026 that no Black woman had ever served as governor of a US state, and that Georgia had never had a woman governor[16]. According to the AP, only three Black governors had previously been elected in US history, all of them Democratic men[3]. The first was L. Douglas Wilder of Virginia, whom the Library of Virginia describes as "the first African American in the United States to be elected governor of a state"; he took office in January 1990[17]. AP reporting carried by France 24 noted that Georgia's 83 previous governors had all been white men[18].

Eight Black major-party nominees for governor were on the 2026 ballot, two of them women: Bottoms and the Minnesota Republican Lisa Demuth[3]. "No matter who you voted for tonight, I will be a governor for all of Georgia," Bottoms told supporters[3]. Jackson conceded, saying: "Tonight, the voters spoke ... and I respect their decision"[3].

In Florida, Donalds beat Democrat David Jolly by 50.0% to 48.7%, or 4,132,112 votes to 4,024,180, according to AP figures compiled by MultiState[9]. The AP described the contest as unexpectedly competitive in a state where Republicans hold a voter-registration advantage of about 1.5 million[8]. "I want you to hear from Florida that we are still red," Donalds told supporters, and he thanked Trump for his support[8]. Because Black candidates from both parties won governorships on the same night, the night's firsts cannot be read as a purely partisan story.

3Congress changes hands and the Speakership awaits a vote

Estimates of the size of the new House majority varied on 4 November because many races had not yet been called. NBC News showed 231 seats called for Democrats, 144 for Republicans and 60 uncalled, and estimated a final Democratic total of about 258 seats, with 29 seats taken from Republicans[7]. In its live coverage, CNBC reported that Democrats were projected to win at least 250 seats, having reported a projection of at least 235 late on election night[5,13]. A House majority requires 218 seats[7,19]. For the Senate, CNBC reported Democrats on track for at least 54 seats and Republicans for at least 42, while MS NOW reported that Democrats had flipped enough Republican-held seats to reach the 51 needed for outright control[13,19].

Voters do not choose the Speaker. CNBC reported that Jeffries would need the support of a majority of the Democratic caucus after the new Congress is seated in January[5]. The Office of the House Historian records 56 people as having served as Speaker since Frederick Augustus Conrad Muhlenberg of Pennsylvania was elected on 1 April 1789, and lists Republican Mike Johnson of Louisiana as the current Speaker[20]. Jeffries represents New York's Eighth District in Brooklyn and, according to his official biography, was unanimously elected Democratic Leader by his colleagues in 2022 and 2024[21].

In New Hampshire, Pappas beat John E. Sununu, a former Republican senator, for the seat left vacant by the retirement of the three-term Democrat Jeanne Shaheen[4]. NBC's results gave Pappas 56.2% to Sununu's 41.9%[6]. Pappas, who is 46, has sat in the House since 2019[4].

There were other firsts in the Senate. According to the AP, Democrat Peggy Flanagan, a citizen of the White Earth Band of Ojibwe, defeated the former NFL sideline reporter Michele Tafoya in Minnesota to become the first Native American woman elected to the Senate[1]. Before the vote, CAWP noted that if Juliana Stratton won in Illinois, three Black women would serve concurrently in the Senate for the first time in US history[22]. In Michigan, where Euronews described a tight race, the AP listed the Democrat Abdul El-Sayed as the first Muslim US senator pending final results and quoted him as saying "You can just go on and call me Senator"[1,2]. NBC's results showed El-Sayed ahead by 52.5% to 45.5%, and a win would make him the first Muslim senator[1,2,6].

4Indian-American candidates gain ground in the House

Pia Dandiya, a 36-year-old Democrat and former teacher and high-school principal, won Florida's newly redrawn 22nd congressional district, defeating the Republican businessman and Marine Corps veteran Casey Askar[23]. According to American Bazaar, a publication covering the Indian-American community, she was born and raised in Palm Beach County to parents who emigrated from India, and Trump carried the district, which spans parts of Palm Beach, Broward, Hendry and Collier counties, by roughly 10 percentage points in 2024[23]. Business Today, an Indian business magazine, put her share of the vote at 51.85%[24]. Both outlets describe her as the first Indian American to represent Florida in the House, and American Bazaar adds that she is the tenth Indian American elected to Congress[23,24].

Suhas Subramanyam, a Democrat and former adviser to President Barack Obama, won a second term in Virginia's 10th district against Republican Dave Beckwith and the independent Ahsen Mujeeb Malik, and Business Today reported his share of the vote at 63.16%[24,25]. Ro Khanna held California's 17th district, with WION, an Indian broadcaster, reporting that he had 72.0% of the votes counted so far[25]. Pramila Jayapal won a sixth term in Washington's 7th district with 87.5%[24,25]. Aruna Miller was re-elected lieutenant governor of Maryland on the ticket of Governor Wes Moore[24,25]. Indian-American Republicans did less well: Saritha Komatireddy lost the race for New York attorney general to Democrat Letitia James, and WION listed Ami Bera's California seat as still pending[25].

Indian Americans have only a short history in Congress. The first Asian American in the House was Dalip Singh Saund, born in Amritsar, who was elected from California in 1956 and served three terms, ending in 1963[26]. Using the Census Bureau's 2022 American Community Survey, the Pew Research Center estimates that about 4.8 million Indian Americans lived in the United States in 2022, around 20% of the Asian-American population[27]. In Pew's survey, 68% of Indian-American registered voters aligned with the Democratic Party and 29% with the Republican Party. The Indian-American sample was 897 of the 7,006 Asian-American adults interviewed between July 2022 and January 2023[27]. That partisan lean fits a 2026 pattern in which Indian-American Democrats won while the most prominent Indian-American Republican lost, though a single election cannot show why.

5Ohio rejects a candidate endorsed by Trump

Acton won 52.0% of the vote (2,350,266 votes) to Ramaswamy's 44.7% (2,023,379), with the Libertarian Don Kissick on 3.3%, a margin of 7.2 percentage points, according to figures compiled by MultiState[12]. Acton, who is 60, is a public health physician who served as Ohio's health director in the cabinet of Republican Governor Mike DeWine during the early Covid-19 pandemic[10,11]. The AP reported that she is the first Democrat to win the Ohio governorship in 20 years and the first woman elected to the post. It added that she will succeed DeWine without any previous experience of elected office[11].

The campaign was expensive and drew national figures. According to the AP, more than $100 million was spent against Acton, and Elon Musk gave at least $5 million to a political action committee aligned with Ramaswamy[11]. Trump and Vice President JD Vance made repeated campaign appearances for Ramaswamy. Ramaswamy announced a $4 million purchase of air time for a controversial attack advertisement but spent about $1 million before withdrawing it about a week before the election[11]. NBC News reported that when Trump endorsed Ramaswamy in October, he acknowledged that Ramaswamy had once opposed him[10].

Ramaswamy, who is 41, is a biotechnology entrepreneur who sought the Republican presidential nomination in 2024[10,11]. NBC News reported that, as an Indian American of Hindu faith running in a mainly white and Christian state, he faced online racism and bigotry and deleted social media apps from his phone[10]. After his defeat he said: "It was a tough race. It was bitter at times, in both directions"[10]. Acton told supporters that "Ohio has chosen unity and decency over division and hate"[10].

Ramaswamy has argued against identity-based appeals in his own party. After Democratic wins in the November 2025 elections, he said Republicans should stop identity-focused political appeals, adding: "We don't care about the colour of your skin or religion; we care about the content of your character"[28]. His defeat fits neither of the main readings of the night. As an Indian-American candidate, he is not part of a story of minority success; as a Trump-endorsed Republican, he was part of the wider Republican defeat. The available sources do not allow his result to be attributed to his identity, his campaign or the national swing.

6Women, Latino and Native candidates and the argument over representation

The results follow a record number of candidacies. CAWP's final nominee data, published on 29 September 2026, counted 26 women nominated for governor, a record 35.1% of all gubernatorial nominees, including 19 Democrats and 7 Republicans[29]. It also counted 271 women nominated for the House (31.8% of nominees) and 19 for the Senate[29]. Before the election, 152 women sat in the 119th Congress, including a record 27 in the Senate[22].

Latino and Native American candidates also achieved firsts. Democrat Gina Hinojosa beat the Republican governor Greg Abbott by 52.9% to 45.6% in Texas and will be the state's first Latina governor[2,30]. In California, Democrat Xavier Becerra beat Republican Steve Hilton by 64.0% to 36.0%[31]. AP reporting carried by France 24 describes Becerra as California's first Latino governor since 1875[18]. According to the California State Library, the governor in 1875, Romualdo Pacheco, was "the first governor of Mexican ancestry" but assumed the office when Newton Booth resigned, and served for about ten months[32]. On that record, Becerra may be the first Latino to be elected governor of California rather than succeed to the office, although none of the sources consulted makes that claim. In New Mexico, Haaland, previously the US Interior Secretary, beat Republican Gregg Hull by 57.2% to 42.8%[2,33].

The make-up of the outgoing Congress gives a point of comparison. Pew found that 26% of the voting members of the 119th Congress identified as a race or ethnicity other than non-Hispanic white, the most diverse Congress to date, while non-Hispanic white people made up 74% of Congress against 58% of the population[34]. Pew found that 84% of members from racial or ethnic minorities were Democrats[34].

Opinions differ on what the firsts mean. Euronews quoted Bill Shiebler of State Voices as saying: "We're witnessing an electorate that increasingly sees itself in its leaders"[2]. Ramaswamy's argument that parties should judge character rather than identity represents the opposing view[28]. Pappas himself said "I'm not running to make history"[1]. In a 1999 paper in The Journal of Politics, the political scientist Jane Mansbridge offered a contingent defence of what scholars call descriptive representation, meaning representatives who share characteristics with the people they represent. She argued that in some contexts "the better communication and experiential knowledge of descriptive representatives enhances their substantive representation"[35].

Uncertainties and open questions

Several of the results cited rest on incomplete counts. On 4 November NBC News still listed 60 House races as uncalled, and published estimates of the Democratic total ranged from at least 235 to about 258 seats[5,7,13]. The Alaska Senate race, in which Democrat Mary Peltola could become the first Alaska Native senator, was too early to call: Republican Dan Sullivan had 48.5% to Peltola's 47.7%, with 72.4% of the expected vote reported[1,6]. Mississippi's Senate race was also uncalled[6,13]. Outlets differed on Michigan. The AP listed El-Sayed as the first Muslim senator pending final results, while Euronews described him as still competing[1,2]. The MultiState figures for Georgia show only two candidates, although CBS News lists the Libertarian Chase Oliver on the ballot, so the published shares may cover only the two main candidates[14,15]. The claim that Dandiya is the tenth Indian American elected to Congress rests on a single diaspora publication[23]. Jeffries's speakership depends on a House vote in January[5]. Pew's figures on the party loyalties of Indian Americans come from 897 respondents surveyed in 2022 and 2023[27].

Conclusion

On the AP's count, the elections of 3 November produced an unusually long list of firsts. They include the first Black woman elected governor, in Georgia; the first openly gay man elected to the Senate, from New Hampshire; the first Native American woman elected governor, in New Mexico; and a probable first Black Speaker of the House[1]. They also produced Florida's first Black governor, a Republican. In Ohio, Ramaswamy, an Indian-American candidate endorsed by Trump, lost to a woman who will be the state's first elected female governor[8,10]. Among Indian-American Democrats, Dandiya won a House seat in Florida and Subramanyam was re-elected in Virginia[23,24]. Because these milestones came with a large swing to the Democrats, it is hard to separate the effect of the candidates' identities from that of the national political mood. Final counts, the January vote for Speaker and the uncalled Senate races in Alaska and Mississippi will settle several open questions[5,6]. Whether the new office holders change what government does will take far longer to judge.
