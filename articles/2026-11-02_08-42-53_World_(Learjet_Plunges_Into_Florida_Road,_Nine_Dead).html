A business jet falls beside a road in Greenacres

A Learjet 60 business jet carrying nine people crashed beside Military Trail, a busy road through the Greenacres area of Palm Beach County, Florida, at about 12:50 pm local time on Sunday 1 November 2026[1,2]. The US Transportation Secretary, Sean Duffy, confirmed that all nine people on board had died and called the crash a "horrific tragedy"[1,3]. The jet had taken off from Austin-Bergstrom International Airport in Texas and was bound for North Eleuthera Airport in the Bahamas[2,4]. Local reporting that quoted officials said that no one on the ground was hurt[4]. The impact left a crater in the roadway and scattered debris close to homes and vehicles[3,4].

Florida's governor, Ron DeSantis, told reporters: "It looked like a missile going down and left a big crater on the side of the road"[1]. Dashboard camera footage broadcast by several outlets showed the jet plunging towards the ground before it erupted into a fireball and sent thick black smoke into the air[5,6]. The National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) is leading the investigation, with support from the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA)[1,4].

The aircraft carried the US registration N722BD[7,8]. The FAA aircraft register lists it as owned by LR60271 LLC of Wilmington, Delaware; that entry records ownership but does not identify who operated the flight[8,9]. The jet was manufactured in 2004, making it 22 years old[10,11]. AeroCorner, summarising CNN's coverage, reported that the jet was listed online as available for charter, but the flight's operator had not been publicly identified[8].

Radio calls and tracking data from the final minutes

Much of the public account of the flight's last minutes comes from two unofficial sources: air traffic control audio archived by LiveATC.net, and position data compiled by flight-tracking services such as Flightradar24[8,12]. Neither is the official record that investigators will use, and transcriptions published by different outlets vary in wording. The aircraft was heading east at 35,000 feet when the pilots reported icing, turbulence and precipitation and asked to climb a further 2,000 feet[1,13].

The pilot told Miami Air Route Traffic Control Center: "We have light, clear ice that is manageable and moderate turbulence"[1,14]. Then came the emergency call: "We're descending 8,000. Mayday, mayday. We're descending 10,000 feet per minute."[1,12]. The pilot next reported, "We are in an overspeed condition descending 20,000 feet per minute."[6]. The controller replied, "I'm here with you. Talk to me."[12]. The last transmissions included "We're gonna crash into the ground"[6,12].

The FAA said that the aircraft descended from 35,250 feet to 8,750 feet in just over one minute[8,15]. AvWeb reported that flight tracking showed a descent exceeding 32,000 feet per minute before radar contact was lost, and AeroTime attributed a similar figure to Flightradar24[13,14], whereas the Associated Press reported a peak of 30,000 feet per minute, which it described as beyond the aircraft's design limits[1]. The differences between these figures may come from different data sources and calculation methods, and investigators have not yet published their own reconstruction.

Thunderstorms, ice and the competing explanations

At 12:54 pm, minutes before the crash, the National Weather Service office in Miami said "Scattered to numerous showers and storms will continue to affect the region" and warned of periods of marginal or instrument flying conditions[16]. AvWeb reported that the Learjet appears to have passed through a stronger thunderstorm cell before its rapid descent began, but said investigators had not determined what part the weather played[14].

Aviation research gives context for what the crew described, but not an explanation. A 2015 conference paper led by Thomas Ratvasky summarised what pilots had seen during engine power-loss events in clouds dense with small ice crystals: "light to moderate turbulence, precipitation on the windscreen (often reported as rain), aircraft total temperature anomalies, lack of significant airframe icing"[17]. A 2014 paper by Jen-Ching Tsao, Peter Struk and Michael Oliver modelled how such crystals, met near deep convective weather, can build up inside turbofan compressors and cause engine power to roll back[18]. The crew's reports of turbulence and precipitation fit that pattern, but their report of clear ice on the airframe does not, and no public transcript mentions engine trouble.

Steve Arroyo, an aviation safety expert quoted by the Associated Press, said that icing and turbulence could have been factors in the crash and may have caused the plane to stall[19]. DeWitt Ingram, a Learjet type-rated captain, said: "If the pilot flew into the tops of one of the thunderstorms, in that very rough air, he could have damaged the airplane to the point where he lost control."[20]. CBS12 noted that neither weather damage nor an in-flight break-up had been established[8,20]. AeroTime reported that analysis of the dashcam footage suggested the Learjet was missing its horizontal stabiliser, the tail surface that controls the aircraft's nose-up and nose-down attitude[13]. This rests on informal review of consumer video, and investigators had not confirmed it.

Officials have been more guarded. Governor DeSantis's remark that "something happened in the air" was an inference from the manner of the descent, not a finding[21]. Aerospace Global News reported that the NTSB is examining weather data, aircraft records, communications and the wreckage[10].

Displaced residents and the timetable of the inquiry

About 600 residents of a mobile home community next to the crash site were evacuated, because its entrance lies beside an area treated as a crime scene during the investigation[1,21]. Palm Beach County Commissioner Joel Flores predicted that the southbound side of Military Trail could remain closed for at least 30 days[21].

The NTSB sent a go-team of investigators and scheduled a media briefing in West Palm Beach for Monday 2 November[4,22]. Under the agency's published process, an investigation moves from notification and on-site fact-gathering to analysis, a final report containing a determination of probable cause, and safety recommendations[23]. The agency says it generally tries to complete an investigation within 12 to 24 months[23].

What remains uncertain

The cause of the crash has not been determined[1,8,14]. Descent rates differ between sources[1,13,14], the radio transcripts come from unofficial recordings archived by LiveATC.net[1,12], and the operator has not been publicly identified[8]. The claim that the horizontal stabiliser was missing has not been verified[13]. The published research on ice crystal icing deals with engine power loss and has not been linked to this flight[17,18].

2A 22-year-old jet on a route from Texas to the Bahamas

The aircraft carried the US registration N722BD and the manufacturer's serial number 60-271, according to aviation outlets that consulted tracking and registry records[8,20]. The FAA aircraft register lists it as owned by LR60271 LLC of Wilmington, Delaware; that entry records ownership but does not identify who operated the flight[8,21]. The jet was manufactured in 2004, making it 22 years old[19,22]. AeroCorner, summarising CNN's coverage, reported that the jet was listed online as available for charter, but the flight's operator had not been publicly identified[8]. Jim Reynolds, a spokesperson for Austin-Bergstrom airport, confirmed that it was a private aircraft but could not immediately give details of who was aboard[5].

Flightradar24 data cited by AeroCorner show that the jet left Austin at 15:37 UTC[8]. The crash occurred at about 17:50 UTC, a little over two hours and ten minutes later[9]. In early reporting, officials had not said how many of the nine were crew members and how many were passengers, and none of the victims had been publicly identified[23,24]. Aerospace Global News reported seven passengers and two crew members, whereas Yahoo News reported that authorities had not disclosed how many were crew and how many were passengers[19,23]. In previous investigations the NTSB has said that it has no role in releasing victims' identities, which is a matter for local authorities[25].

The Learjet 60 is a mid-size business jet powered by two Pratt & Whitney Canada PW305A turbofan engines, each producing about 4,600 pounds of thrust[8,26]. It first flew on 10 October 1990 and received FAA certification in January 1993[26]. Wikipedia records 429 aircraft built, with the original Model 60 produced until 2007 and an updated 60XR version after that[26]. The type's earlier fatal accidents include one on 19 September 2008 at Columbia, South Carolina, when a Learjet 60 registered N999LJ and operated by Global Exec Aviation crashed during a rejected take-off; four people died and two survived, among them the musician Travis Barker[27]. The NTSB found that greatly underinflated tyres and a rejected take-off after the decision speed known as V1 were the probable causes[28]. That accident happened on the ground and has no evident connection with a loss of control at cruising altitude.

3Radio calls and tracking data from the final minutes

Much of the public account of the flight's last minutes comes from two unofficial sources: air traffic control audio archived by LiveATC.net, and ADS-B position data compiled by flight-tracking services such as Flightradar24[6,8,10]. Neither is the official record that investigators will use, and transcriptions published by different outlets vary in wording. According to ADS-B data described by AvWeb, the jet climbed to 41,000 feet after departure and later descended near Naples, on Florida's west coast[10]. It was heading east at 35,000 feet when the pilots reported icing, turbulence and precipitation and asked to climb a further 2,000 feet[1,11]. AeroTime reported that the aircraft had strayed up to 700 feet from its assigned altitude before that request[11].

The pilot told Miami Air Route Traffic Control Center: "We have light, clear ice that is manageable and moderate turbulence"[1,10]. Fox 13's transcription of the same recording includes an outside air temperature of minus 47 degrees Celsius and the words "We're managing the ice, but it is moderate turbulence", followed by "Now we're picking up precipitation. We need to climb."[6]. Then came the emergency call: "We're descending 8,000. Mayday, mayday. We're descending 10,000 feet per minute."[1,6]. The pilot next reported, "We are in an overspeed condition descending 20,000 feet per minute," and, in Fox 13's transcription, "We are descending 22,000 feet, and we are banking 60 degrees."[1,5,6]. The controller replied, "I'm here with you. Talk to me."[6]. The last transmissions included "We're gonna crash into the ground" and, according to CBS Miami, "We're out of time"[5,6].

The FAA said that the aircraft descended from 35,250 feet to 8,750 feet in just over one minute[8,9]. Latin Times put that at roughly 350 feet per second[22]. AvWeb reported that flight tracking showed a descent exceeding 32,000 feet per minute before radar contact was lost, and AeroTime attributed a similar figure to Flightradar24[10,11], whereas the Associated Press reported a peak of 30,000 feet per minute, which it described as beyond the aircraft's design limits[1]. The last recorded altitude, 8,750 feet, was logged at 17:49:22 UTC[19]. The differences between these figures may come from different data sources and calculation methods, and investigators have not yet published their own reconstruction.

4Thunderstorms over South Florida and the hazard of high-altitude ice

The weather on 1 November was poor. At 5:38 am the National Weather Service (NWS) office in Miami forecast that "showers and storms are expected to be widespread today and tomorrow" and noted a flood watch for eastern South Florida through Monday afternoon[29]. Its update at 12:54 pm, minutes before the crash, said "Scattered to numerous showers and storms will continue to affect the region" and described a shortwave moving into the eastern Gulf "assisting with the development of deep convection"[7]. The aviation section warned of periods of marginal or instrument flying conditions and easterly winds of 10 to 15 knots gusting to 20 to 25 knots[7]. AvWeb reported that the Learjet appears to have passed through a stronger thunderstorm cell before its rapid descent began, but said investigators had not determined what part the weather played[10].

Aviation research gives context for what the crew described, but not an explanation. A 2015 conference paper by researchers from NASA, France's CNRS, Airbus and other bodies, led by Thomas Ratvasky, summarised what pilots had seen during engine power-loss events in clouds dense with small ice crystals: "light to moderate turbulence, precipitation on the windscreen (often reported as rain), aircraft total temperature anomalies, lack of significant airframe icing", and nothing showing on weather radar[30]. A 2014 paper by Jen-Ching Tsao, Peter Struk and Michael Oliver modelled how such crystals, met near deep convective weather, can build up inside turbofan compressors and cause engine power to roll back[31]. Both were conference papers, not journal articles. The crew's reports of turbulence and precipitation fit that pattern, but their report of clear ice on the airframe does not, and no public transcript mentions engine trouble.

Regulators have treated high-altitude ice as a certification issue for more than a decade. In November 2014 the FAA amended its airworthiness standards for certain transport category aeroplanes and aircraft engines to cover supercooled large drop, mixed phase and ice crystal icing conditions[32]. The rulemaking record mentions an earlier airworthiness directive for the Beechjet 400A, a business jet, issued after "reports of dual engine flameouts in high altitude icing conditions believed to include ice crystals"[32].

One widely studied case is Air France flight 447, an Airbus A330 that crashed into the Atlantic on 1 June 2009, killing all 228 people aboard[33]. France's investigation bureau, the BEA, reported in July 2012 that the speed sensors may have frozen over and given false readings, after which a co-pilot pitched the nose up instead of down, and the aircraft did not recover[33]. Whether anything similar happened over Florida is unknown.

5Early explanations from pilots, consultants and officials

Without official findings, commentators have put forward several hypotheses, and these are not mutually exclusive. Steve Arroyo, an aviation safety expert quoted by the Associated Press, said that icing and turbulence could have been factors in the crash and may have caused the plane to stall[13]. On that account, the build-up of ice and rough air at high altitude would have degraded the wing's ability to produce lift, and the crew would then have been unable to stop the descent before the aircraft exceeded its limits. That interpretation is consistent with the crew's reports of ice and turbulence, but nothing published so far confirms it.

DeWitt Ingram, a Learjet type-rated captain and founder of the consultancy Par-Avion Group, examined flight-tracking data and videos for the broadcaster CBS12[14]. He said: "If the pilot flew into the tops of one of the thunderstorms, in that very rough air, he could have damaged the airplane to the point where he lost control."[14]. He attributed the lack of recognisable wreckage, such as an intact tail or cabin, to the speed of impact, saying: "The energy transfer breaks apart the aircraft fuselage."[14]. CBS12 noted that neither weather damage nor an in-flight break-up had been established[8,14].

A third line of discussion came from the dashcam video. AeroTime reported that analysis of the footage suggested the Learjet was missing its horizontal stabiliser, the tail surface that controls the aircraft's nose-up and nose-down attitude[11]. This rests on informal review of consumer video, and investigators had not confirmed it. Even if correct, the footage alone could not show whether the tail failed first or broke away later in the dive. Tail parts found away from the main impact site would help answer that question.

Officials have been more guarded. Governor DeSantis's remark that "something happened in the air" was an inference from the manner of the descent, not a finding[12]. Aerospace Global News reported that the NTSB is examining weather data, aircraft records, communications and the wreckage, and International Business Times reported that the inquiry will also cover icing, flight control systems and maintenance history[19,34]. The radio calls, the weather record and the descent profile each fit more than one sequence of events, and every hypothesis so far rests on public data rather than on examination of the wreckage.

6Displaced residents and the timetable of a federal inquiry

About 600 residents of a mobile home community next to the crash site were evacuated, because its entrance lies beside an area treated as a crime scene during the investigation[1,12]. Palm Beach County Commissioner Joel Flores said: "So we have about 600 people that we have to make sure that they have shelter tonight."[16]. Officials opened two emergency shelters, and the county's Palm Tran buses were ready to move residents once law enforcement allowed it[2]. Evacuees were directed to the Greenacres Community Center on Swain Boulevard, and John I. Leonard High School closed on Monday as emergency operations continued[3]. The Red Cross provided support to displaced residents[16,24].

The damage to the road will take time to repair. Flores said: "That's a massive crater on Military. That's gonna take some time to fix," and predicted that the southbound side could remain closed for at least 30 days[12]. CBS12 reported that Military Trail could stay closed between Cresthaven Boulevard and 10th Avenue North for weeks[3]. Mitchell Wilson, who was about five blocks away when the jet came down, described seeing "damaged cars and gravel and pieces of the road sticking up out of the ground"[21]. Bishop Manuel de Jesús Rodríguez announced a memorial Mass for the nine victims at 10 am on Monday at Our Lady Queen of Peace Cemetery in Royal Palm Beach[3].

The NTSB sent a go-team of investigators and scheduled a media briefing in West Palm Beach for Monday 2 November[3,23]. Under the agency's published process, an investigation moves from notification and on-site fact-gathering to analysis, a final report containing a determination of probable cause, and safety recommendations; the NTSB can also designate other organisations as parties to the investigation to draw on their resources[15]. The agency says it generally tries to complete an investigation within 12 to 24 months[15]. For the January 2025 crash of a Learjet 55 medical evacuation flight in Philadelphia, the NTSB said a preliminary report containing factual information would be expected within 30 days, and that probable cause would appear in a final report expected within 12 to 24 months[35]. If the same timetable applies here, a factual preliminary report would be due in early December 2026, with a determination of cause in late 2027 or 2028.

Uncertainties and open questions

The cause of the crash has not been determined. Several basic figures differ between sources. The Associated Press reported a peak descent rate of 30,000 feet per minute, while flight-tracking data reported by AvWeb exceeded 32,000 feet per minute[1,10]. The radio transcripts come from unofficial recordings archived by LiveATC.net, and outlets word the same calls differently: the Associated Press quoted "light, clear ice that is manageable", whereas Fox 13 transcribed "We're managing the ice"[1,6]. Aerospace Global News reported a split of seven passengers and two crew, while Yahoo News said authorities had not disclosed one[19,23]. The operator and whether the flight was a charter remain unconfirmed; the charter listing is known only through a secondary summary of CNN's reporting[8]. The claim that the horizontal stabiliser was missing comes from informal video analysis reported by AeroTime and has not been verified[11]. Two outlets called the crash the second-deadliest US aviation accident of 2026, one citing NTSB records[22,34]. The published research on ice crystal icing deals with engine power loss and has not been linked to this flight[30,31].

Conclusion

The established facts are few. A 22-year-old Learjet 60 flying from Austin to the Bahamas fell from about 35,000 feet to 8,750 feet in little more than a minute and struck a roadside in Greenacres, killing all nine people aboard[1,8,9]. Amid forecast showers and storms, its crew had reported ice, moderate turbulence and precipitation before declaring a mayday and reporting an overspeed condition[1,5,6,7]. Experts have suggested several explanations, including a stall brought on by ice, damage from storms and possible loss of the tail, but none has yet been tested against the wreckage[11,13,14]. The NTSB's final report, which generally takes 12 to 24 months to complete, will set out a determination of probable cause[15]. For the 600 residents forced from their homes, the consequences began straight away[1].
