A shooting in the heart of Kraków

Shortly after 3 p.m. on Saturday 7 November 2026, a man drove a car onto the Main Market Square in Kraków, Poland's second-largest city, and opened fire on people at random[1,2,3]. Six people died in the square, four women and two men, and about 15 were wounded, according to the city authorities[2]. That evening Prime Minister Donald Tusk said the bodies of two women and a man had been found at the attacker's home and that there was much to suggest they were members of his immediate family[1,4]. News agencies described the attack as Poland's deadliest shooting since the fall of communism in 1989[1,3].

According to a reconstruction by the news portal Wirtualna Polska, the gunman fired at pedestrians near the Cloth Hall, then drove towards Wawel and fired at a line 1 tram on All Saints' Square[5]. Police officers shot the attacker and detained him at the junction of Dietla and Stradomska streets, about one kilometre from the square[2,6]. Early reports of a second gunman proved false, and police said the attacker was a Polish citizen and the only perpetrator[6].

Victims, the wounded and the police response

By the night of 7 November, four of the six people killed in the square had been identified: three Polish women and a British man, while two victims had not yet been identified[7,8]. A US State Department spokesperson told CBS News that it was not aware of any American citizens being harmed[9].

Counts of the wounded differ. The city authorities gave a figure of 15[2]. Radio Kraków reported 16, a total that included two police officers and the attacker himself[8]. Reuters reported 17 wounded, including two police officers[10]. The news portal Interia reported that 12 of the injured had been classed as critical at triage[11].

According to the Małopolska police, as reported by WP, the first patrol began to act 23 seconds after the first call[5]. The interior minister, Marcin Kierwiński, said officers fired their first shots at the attacker three minutes after the report[12]. Radio Kraków reported that senior constable Jakub Kulik was in a critical condition with three severe wounds and a damaged lung, while senior constable Artur Morański was in a better condition[8].

A licensed sports shooter and an unresolved question over the rifle

The city authorities described the attacker as a Polish citizen with no criminal record who held a valid firearms permit[2]. Polish media identified him only as Michał O., born in 2002, from Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski, a town north-east of Kraków[13,14,15]. Rzeczpospolita reported that he worked as a lathe operator[12]. The defence minister, Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz, categorically denied reports that he was or had ever been a soldier of the Polish army[7,16].

Interia and WP reported that Michał O. held a sports-shooting permit with two firearms registered in his name and trained at the Świt club in Starachowice, and that his father, Janusz O., a member of the same club, had 11 registered firearms[5,14]. France 24 and Al Jazeera, drawing on agency reports, described the weapon as a semi-automatic rifle similar to the American AR-15, whereas NBC News quoted Kierwiński as saying the attacker had used an automatic rifle[1,3,15]. Kierwiński said the working hypothesis was that the attacker had used his father's legally registered weapon, but stressed that this was unconfirmed and needed to be verified[17].

At the attacker's home, police found the bodies of two women and a man[4,15]. The legal news site Bezprawnik noted that the authorities had not formally confirmed the identities of the dead or the circumstances of their deaths[18].

Strict permit rules and weak oversight after issue

Polish firearms law is restrictive on paper: applicants must give a recognised reason and pass medical and psychological examinations, criminal record checks and theoretical and practical tests[19]. Poland had about 2.5 firearms per 100 inhabitants, the lowest rate in the EU, according to a February 2026 report by Polskie Radio[20]. Ownership is nonetheless rising fast: the police issued a record 50,709 new permits in 2025, 10.6% more than in 2024[21]. The number of valid permits rose from 192,819 in 2015 to 411,769 in 2025, and privately owned registered firearms passed one million[21].

In July 2026 the Supreme Audit Office (NIK) published an audit which found that hunters, sports shooters and collectors are assessed medically and psychologically only once, when they obtain a permit that is then valid indefinitely, and that there was no mechanism to check whether holders still met the conditions of issue[22]. NIK said this was contrary to EU requirements[22].

On 9 January 2025 the Sejm rejected at first reading a Poland 2050 bill that would have required periodic medical examinations for hunters, by 225 votes to 203 with one abstention[23]. Edward Siarka of Law and Justice said the checks would cost about 1,000 złoty per person[23]. The deputy Andrzej Zapałowski argued that resources should go to fighting illegal weapons[24].

Motive, terrorism and what remains uncertain

Kierwiński said: "Everything indicates he was fascinated by far-right ideologies"[15]. Rzeczpospolita quoted him as saying that the first analysis pointed to ultranationalist views but that this would be verified[12], and he said investigators had not yet established whether those views had motivated the attack[17]. Tusk called the attack terrorism, and Kosiniak-Kamysz called it an act of terror[1,12]. According to wnp.pl, seven prosecutors were assigned to the case, and the Internal Security Agency and the Military Counterintelligence Service were examining his background, contacts and possible foreign disinformation, with no confirmation of an organised group or outside help[4].

Roman Polko, a former commander of the GROM special forces unit, asked how such a person had been able to prepare all this when practically no one paid attention[25].

Much therefore remains unsettled. The motive rests on preliminary assessments by the interior minister[12,17]. The origin and legal status of the rifle are not established[17,19]. Sources disagree on the attacker's age, 24 or 25[5,9], and on the number of wounded, from 15 to 17[2,8,10]. The identities of the three people found at his home and the circumstances of their deaths had not been formally confirmed[18].

1Gunfire across Kraków's medieval market square

Shortly after 3 p.m. on Saturday 7 November 2026, a man drove a car onto the Main Market Square in Kraków, Poland's second-largest city, and opened fire on people at random[1,2,3]. Six people died in the square, four women and two men, and about 15 were wounded, according to the city authorities[2]. That evening, after a briefing with the security services, Prime Minister Donald Tusk said the bodies of two women and a man had been found at the attacker's home and that there was much to suggest they were members of his immediate family[1,4]. Tusk called the attack terrorism, saying: "Until now in Poland, we haven't had such drastic cases of terrorism"[1]. France 24 reported him as saying that Poland had never experienced anything quite like it[15]. News agencies described the attack as Poland's deadliest shooting since the fall of communism in 1989[1,3].

The shooting moved through the old town rather than staying in one place. According to a reconstruction by the news portal Wirtualna Polska (WP), the gunman fired at pedestrians near the Cloth Hall, a Renaissance building on the square, then drove towards Wawel and fired at a line 1 tram on All Saints' Square[16]. The city authorities said the window of the tram driver's cabin was shattered but that neither the driver nor any passenger was hurt[2]. Police officers shot the attacker and detained him at the junction of Dietla and Stradomska streets, about one kilometre from the square, according to the city and the daily Rzeczpospolita[2,5]. Early reports of a second gunman proved false, and police denied them, saying the attacker was a Polish citizen and the only perpetrator[5].

The setting explains much of the shock. The square was laid out anew in 1257 after the Mongol invasion of 1241, covers 3.79 hectares and is sometimes called one of the largest medieval town squares in Europe; it is part of the Historic Centre of Kraków, which became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1978[17]. The governor of the Małopolska region, Krzysztof Jan Klęczar, said that the city, always seen as extremely safe, had proved not to be free of such terrible acts[18]. Radio Kraków reported that the hejnał, the city's trumpet call, fell silent for 12 hours, for the first time since 1939[7]. For a country with few mass shootings, an attack in its best-known public space has carried national weight.

2Victims, the wounded and a three-minute police response

By the night of 7 November, four of the six people killed in the square had been identified: three Polish women and a British man, while two victims had not yet been identified[7,19]. The national police commander, Marek Boroń, confirmed that three of the dead were Polish citizens and one was British[20]. The UK foreign secretary, Ed Miliband, said: "The UK Government deeply mourns the British national and other victims who have died after the appalling attack in Krakow"[20]. The British prime minister, Andy Burnham, said he was shocked and saddened[20]. A US State Department spokesperson told CBS News that it was not aware of any American citizens being harmed[21].

Counts of the wounded differ according to the source and to whom they include. The city authorities gave a figure of 15[2]. Radio Kraków, the regional public broadcaster, reported 16, a total that included two police officers and the attacker himself, of whom 11 were Polish and five were foreigners from Britain, Portugal, Russia, Germany and Austria[7]. Reuters reported 17 wounded, including two police officers[8]. The news portal Interia reported that 12 of the injured had been classed as critical at triage, and the Kyiv Post reported that 12 remained in a life-threatening condition after emergency surgery[18,22]. The differences most probably reflect counts made at different times and on different definitions, rather than a substantive dispute.

The speed of the police response is central to the official account. According to the Małopolska police, as reported by WP, the first patrol began to act 23 seconds after the first call[16]. The interior minister, Marcin Kierwiński, said officers fired their first shots at the attacker three minutes after the report[6]. The business portal wnp.pl reported that 30 police units and 24 fire brigade units took part in the operation[4]. The two timings measure different things, the start of the response and the first exchange of fire, and do not contradict each other.

Two officers were shot during the arrest. Radio Kraków reported that senior constable Jakub Kulik was in a critical condition with three severe wounds and a damaged lung, while senior constable Artur Morański was in a better condition, and that both had undergone surgery at the Narutowicz hospital[7]. Tusk said: "Senior constable Jakub Kulik and senior constable Artur Moranski are true heroes"[9]. He added that "Our highest praise goes to the heroic police officers who risked their lives"[3]. The ministry spokesperson, Karolina Gałecka, described the police action as fast and efficient[7].

3A licensed sports shooter from Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski

The city authorities described the attacker as a Polish citizen with no criminal record who held a valid firearms permit[2]. Polish media identified him only as Michał O., born in 2002, from Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski, a town north-east of Kraków[9,23,24]. Polish media gave his age as 24, while some English-language reports, including CBS News and a British national news report carried by the Times & Star, gave 25[16,20,21,25]. Rzeczpospolita reported that he worked as a lathe operator[6]. He wore military-style clothing, and Interia reported that he also wore a helmet and body armour[3,24]. The defence minister, Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz, categorically denied reports that he was or had ever been a soldier of the Polish army[19,26].

Accounts of the weapon differ in one important respect. France 24 and Al Jazeera, drawing on agency reports, described it as a semi-automatic rifle similar to the American AR-15[1,3]. NBC News, however, quoted Kierwiński as saying the attacker had used an automatic rifle[9]. The car, a Subaru according to Rzeczpospolita, was registered to the attacker's father, according to Interia[5,23]. WP reported that police recovered several firearms, hundreds of rounds of ammunition and bayonets from it[16]. Interia and WP reported that Michał O. held a sports-shooting permit with two firearms registered in his name and trained at the Świt club in Starachowice, and that his father, Janusz O., a member of the same club, had 11 registered firearms[16,24].

Where the rifle came from has not been established. Kierwiński said the working hypothesis was that the attacker had used his father's legally registered weapon, but stressed that this was unconfirmed and needed to be verified[10]. Writing on the day of the attack, the legal commentator Jerzy Wilczek noted that nobody yet knew whether the weapon was legal[27]. Kierwiński said the attacker's own condition was serious and that a second operation had begun[6].

At the attacker's home, police found the bodies of two women and a man[4,9]. Radio Kraków reported that they were his parents and sister, while NBC News described them as believed to be his parents and sister[7,9]. The legal news site Bezprawnik noted that the authorities had not formally confirmed the identities of the dead or the circumstances of their deaths[14]. Interia reported that counter-terrorism units had carried out operations at his residence[23]. How and when the three died, and whether those deaths came before the drive to Kraków, are among the most important questions for investigators.

4Strict rules on paper and a fast-growing stock of guns

Polish firearms law is restrictive on paper. Applicants must give a recognised reason, such as sport, collecting, hunting or personal protection, and pass medical and psychological examinations, criminal record checks and theoretical and practical tests; a permit for personal protection requires proof of a constant, real and exceptional threat[27]. Owners must store weapons and ammunition so as to prevent access by unauthorised people[14]. Poland had about 2.5 firearms per 100 inhabitants, the lowest rate in the EU, according to a February 2026 report by Polskie Radio, the state-owned public broadcaster[28].

Ownership is nonetheless rising fast. According to police statistics reported by Notes from Poland, the police issued a record 50,709 new permits in 2025, 10.6% more than in 2024, after a jump from 19,939 in 2021 to 37,402 in 2022, the year of Russia's invasion of Ukraine[12]. The number of valid permits rose from 192,819 in 2015 to 411,769 in 2025, and privately owned registered firearms passed one million, compared with about 380,000 in 2014[12]. Polskie Radio gave the total as 1,037,778 firearms, with 21,071 collector permits, 17,601 sports permits and 7,254 personal-protection permits issued in 2025[28]. Sports and collecting permits made up 76% of new licences that year[12].

The state's own auditors had already warned about oversight. In July 2026 the Supreme Audit Office (NIK) published an audit, covering January 2022 to August 2025, which found that hunters, sports shooters and collectors are assessed medically and psychologically only once, when they obtain a permit that is then valid indefinitely, and that there was no mechanism to check whether holders still met the conditions of issue[11]. NIK said this was contrary to EU requirements[11]. Directive (EU) 2021/555 requires member states to have a monitoring system, continuous or not, to ensure that the conditions of authorisation are met throughout its duration and that "relevant medical and psychological information is assessed"[29]. The auditors also found that the police had changed the description of published data from numbers of persons to numbers of permits only after the audit[11].

Attempts to tighten the rules have failed before. On 9 January 2025 the Sejm, the lower house of parliament, rejected at first reading a Poland 2050 bill that would have required periodic medical examinations for hunters, by 225 votes to 203 with one abstention, after the PSL, Law and Justice (PiS) and Confederation groups asked for its rejection[13]. In the debate, Edward Siarka of PiS said the checks would cost about 1,000 złoty per person, and the deputy Andrzej Zapałowski argued that the bill singled out hunters unfairly and that resources should go to fighting illegal weapons[13,30]. In November 2025 an animal-rights group, Workshop for All Beings, complained to the European Commission that Poland was breaching the directive by not requiring periodic checks for hunters[31].

5Ultranationalism, terrorism and the limits of early evidence

Officials moved quickly to describe a possible motive, while stressing that it was unverified. Kierwiński said: "Everything indicates he was fascinated by far-right ideologies"[9]. He also said the attacker had very, very radical views[19,26]. Rzeczpospolita quoted him as saying that the first analysis pointed to ultranationalist views but that this would be verified[6], and he said investigators had not yet established whether those views had motivated the attack[10]. Reuters quoted him as saying that the far-right influence would also be subject to investigation[8]. Interia reported that the attacker had posted about weapons on social media[24].

The government framed the attack as terrorism from the first evening. Notes from Poland quoted Kierwiński as saying "we are dealing with a terrorist attack", and Kosiniak-Kamysz called it an act of terror and appealed for national unity[6,19]. At this stage the label is a political and investigative assessment, not a court finding; no charges had been publicly reported by the morning of 8 November. According to wnp.pl, seven prosecutors were assigned to the case, and the Internal Security Agency (ABW) and the Military Counterintelligence Service (SKW) were examining his background, contacts and possible foreign disinformation, with no confirmation of an organised group or outside help[4]. Rzeczpospolita reported that police cybercrime specialists were analysing his online activity[6].

Completed right-wing terrorist attacks have been rare in the European Union. Europol's EU Terrorism Situation and Trend Report for 2025, covering 2024, recorded a single completed right-wing attack in the whole EU, reported by Italy, against 24 jihadist and 21 left-wing and anarchist attacks[32]. The report noted that the number of minors and young people involved in terrorism continued to rise and that young perpetrators "were predominantly male and had most often undergone a process of self-radicalisation online"[32]. Europol's counts depend on what member states classify and report as terrorism, which limits comparisons between countries.

Others frame the case around warning signs rather than ideology. Roman Polko, a former commander of the GROM special forces unit, asked how such a person had been able to prepare all this when practically no one paid attention, and suggested the military clothing might reflect posing as an action hero rather than any military link[33]. Earlier, on 8, 9 and 10 October 2026, a run of attacks with knives and sharp objects by young people at schools and a college injured students and staff and killed a school custodian[34]. Tusk had denounced those attacks as a "cult of violence"[3]. The two sets of events differ in method and, so far as is known, in motive.

6Mourning, political appeals and the argument over guns

President Karol Nawrocki signed an ordinance after 22:00 on 7 November declaring national mourning on 8 and 9 November, his spokesman Rafał Leśkiewicz announced[25]. Kraków's mayor, Łukasz Gibała, declared mourning in the city until 11 November[2]. According to Rzeczpospolita, Tusk appealed to politicians of all parties not to play politics with the tragedy, and the leader of the opposition PiS party, Jarosław Kaczyński, called for protests to be cancelled out of respect for the mourning[6]. The former president Andrzej Duda, a native of Kraków, offered his condolences[6].

Foreign leaders sent messages of solidarity, among them the European Commission president, Ursula von der Leyen, the German chancellor, Friedrich Merz, and the Ukrainian president, Volodymyr Zelensky[19]. Rzeczpospolita also listed messages from Italy's Giorgia Meloni, France's Emmanuel Macron and the governments of Hungary, Denmark, Romania, Lithuania and others[6]. The US ambassador to Poland, Tom Rose, said: "America stands with Poland at this dark hour as Poland stands with her beloved citizens of Krakow"[21]. The Kyiv Post reported that Ukraine's foreign ministry confirmed that no Ukrainian citizens had been killed or injured[22].

The argument over firearms began within hours, picking up a debate that predated the attack. In February 2026 the centrist Poland 2050 party called for stricter laws that could raise the cost of a permit and tighten the criteria for obtaining one[35]. Polskie Radio reported at the time that experts and politicians were calling for stricter oversight and medical checks[28]. Writing for Bezprawnik on the day of the attack, Wilczek described a quarrel over whether an armed civilian could have stopped the gunman; he argued that civilian fire would risk hitting bystanders and that police could not tell a defender from an attacker, and warned that a debate about permits would miss the point if the weapon turned out to be illegal[27].

A further strand of argument concerns households rather than individuals. Bezprawnik's Mariusz Lewandowski argued that the system sees each applicant when a permit is issued but cannot assess homes where several authorised people keep many firearms[14]. That critique matches NIK's finding that there was no mechanism to check whether holders continue to meet the conditions of their permits[11]. Against this, opponents of the 2025 bill argued that repeated checks would burden law-abiding owners, price out rural residents and divert attention from illegal weapons[13,30]. Which side gains ground is likely to depend on whether investigators confirm that the rifle came from a legally registered collection.

Uncertainties and open questions

Several central facts remain unsettled. The motive rests on preliminary assessments by the interior minister, who said the ultranationalist hypothesis still had to be verified and that it was not yet established whether those views drove the attack[6,10]. The origin and legal status of the rifle are not established; the use of the father's weapon is a hypothesis[10,27]. Sources disagree on whether the rifle was semi-automatic or automatic[3,9], on the attacker's age, 24 or 25[16,21], on the number wounded, from 15 to 17[2,7,8], and on the distance to his home town, about 93 miles according to NBC News and about 200 kilometres according to Reuters[8,9]. Two of the six people killed in the square had not been publicly identified[19]. The identities of the three people found at his home and the circumstances of their deaths had not been formally confirmed[14]. Many details about the suspect, including his club membership and his father's 11 firearms, come from Polish media reports rather than official statements[16,24]. The gun ownership figures come from police registers, whose verification NIK criticised[11].

Conclusion

What is established is that a lone gunman killed six people in one of Europe's best-known public squares on 7 November, that three people believed to be his relatives were found dead at his home, and that police stopped him within minutes[2,4,6]. The Polish government treats the attack as terrorism and points to ultranationalist views, but has said that this account is still to be verified[1,6]. The attack also tests a firearms system that is strict when a permit is issued but, according to the state's own auditors, weak at checking owners afterwards[11]. Several questions remain open: whether the rifle was legally held, how the attacker came to his views and whether anyone could have seen warning signs. The answers will decide whether the political response centres on extremism, on gun licensing, or on both. They depend on an investigation by prosecutors and the security services that had only just begun.
